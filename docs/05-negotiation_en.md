# 05 · Qualifying (Negotiation)

> English version of [05-negotiation.md](05-negotiation.md). Translated from the Chinese original; figures, dates, commands and file names match the original exactly.

> What this step has to get, in one sentence: the three-piece set — specs, quantity, destination port (including trade terms) — plus three things they may not want to say out loud: target price range, expected payment terms, and who they buy from now. Get them to say it themselves: "what you want, how much, where it goes, on what terms, and who you buy from now".
> The principle, also in one sentence: every question you skip before quoting costs you one chip of bargaining room later. The more precisely you ask, the less you give away and the more accurately you earn — not because you calculate well, but because you stop digging your own hole with a blind quote.
> What this step does NOT do: no quoting, no revealing floor-price costs, no verbal promises on lead time or payment terms, and no offering free samples / moulds / payment terms just to keep them around. Those come in [06-quote_en.md](06-quote_en.md).

## 1. Why this step comes first

Many beginners run their quotation flow like this: the customer says "send me your price list", you send the company price list, then you wait for them to cut it.

The result: they take your price list to other suppliers to compare, and you still do not know what sizes they actually want, how many, or who it ships to. What you quoted was a catalogue price, not a version tailored to them. When it comes to negotiating you have nothing to stand on — you showed your hand before the game started.

Flip the order and it works: get them to spell out their requirements first, then quote. That simple — but most beginners do it backwards.

And qualifying is not just a technical matter; it is a filtering matter and a relationship matter at the same time:
- A real buyer can say very specifically what specs, quantities and destination they need
- A buyer who is not serious will only say "best price, send me quotation"
- A buyer with decision power will discuss the end-user demand in their market with you
- A price-shopper will dodge your questions and keep asking for the lowest price

So this round of conversation is itself a second round of due diligence.

Memorize one rule: quoting always comes after this step, never as the answer to this step. The first time they ask for a price, your first reaction is to ask back, not to quote.

## 2. Step by step

**Step 1: Confirm identity and authority first (do it in the first sentence)**

Do not open by asking about requirements. In the first round, confirm three things — they can sit naturally inside the greeting:
- "Before we go further, could you tell me your role in the company — are you handling purchasing, or is there a colleague I should include?"
- "Are you the importer of record, or do you buy through a trading company?"

Why these two questions matter:
- Confirm whether the person you are talking to can make the decision; if not, find the person who can
- Many markets have a two-layer structure (importer + second-tier wholesaler); only once you know it do you know who the quote goes to and who their downstream is

**Step 2: Ask about specs (multiple choice, not open questions)**

Beginners love asking "What specs do you need?", and the other side answers "all kinds" and you are stuck.

Give them options instead:
- Use: TBR intercity / long-haul logistics / construction and mining / heavy-duty trucks?
- Give three common sizes: 12.00R20, 315/80R22.5, 11.00R20 — "Which sizes do you actually run? I'll quote against the sizes you really use"
- Pattern preference: any specified pattern, any model already in use
- Brand requirements: Chinese brands only / specified pattern / must be their own brand
- Certification and labelling: the certification names the target market requires, and the label language requirements (**the specific certification and labelling rules are as per the target country's latest official requirements**)

Ask 3–4 at a time; do not throw 12 questions at them at once.

**Step 3: Ask about quantity (it decides whether you can give the right price)**

Quantity information is more sensitive than price, because it decides your cost structure:

- How many pieces per single size to start
- Roughly how many pieces fit in one container (20GP / 40HQ) — you must work this out yourself first; do not ask the customer
- They say "500 pieces" — ask back: split across how many sizes? Concentrated on 12.00R20 or 315/80R22.5?
- If one size has too small a quantity, your production scheduling and freight allocation both get worse, and the price naturally cannot be calculated as a big order

How to work out container loading in practice (example: 40HQ loaded with TBR):
- First calculate the packaging volume (length × width × height × number of cartons) and confirm whether the 40HQ's usable volume can hold it (**for the official standards on volume and payload, go by the data published by the carrier and the shipping line**)
- Then calculate the weight per piece and check whether the total weight exceeds the limit
- Only when both pass is it a workable loading plan
- Different specs mean very different volumes per piece; mixed loading saves more space than a single size, but is harder to check

**Step 4: Ask about the destination port and trade terms**

- "Which port? Jebel Ali / Dammam / Jeddah?" — freight differs a lot by destination port
- "Do you need CFR or FOB? And which port do you normally use for pickup?"
- Saudi customers commonly use CIF Dammam / CIF Jeddah; Dubai re-export commonly uses FOB Yantian / Ningbo → CFR Jebel Ali
- Also confirm whether the customer needs insurance and whether they need customs-clearance support (they usually have their own customs broker)

**Step 5: Ask about volume and timetable (to judge real vs fake)**

- "How many containers per month in peak season?" — a real buyer can answer the rhythm; a price-shopper answers "depends"
- "Any deadline — certification deadline, shipment window, or a delivery date your customer needs?" — only time pressure makes a deal possible
- "When would you place the first trial order?"

**Step 6: Give them a small ask (do not start by satisfying them)**

Do not end the conversation by asking "Anything else you need?" — give them a small hook instead:
- "I'll work out a version based on what you said; I'll get the spec sheet sorted and send it to you in the next couple of days."
- "How about I put together a 12.00R20 proposal first, and you check whether the direction is right?"
- "Shall I send you a sample to test first? Once specs are confirmed we can talk quantity and price."

What the small ask does: it turns a conversation of "asking a pile of questions" into a conversation that "has a next step". At the same time you are using their reaction to verify whether they are real — a real buyer will cooperate on testing a sample; a price-shopper will only ask for the lowest price.

**Step 7: Write it up as a requirement confirmation sheet (send it back before quoting)**

Summarize in one sentence, repeat back what they said and get them to confirm:
> Just to confirm: 3 sizes (12.00R20 / 315/80R22.5 / 11.00R20), 1x40HQ mixed loading, destination Dammam, CFR, your own labels. Please correct anything wrong.

This is the single most cost-effective action in the whole flow — confirming information, filtering real from fake, and keeping a written record, all in one. Most beginners skip it, and only start slapping their thighs when they get bounced back with "I thought you meant a different size".

**Step 8: Run through the "nine questions before quoting" checklist (do not move to quoting with any missing)**

Steps 1–7 were about communication moves; this step is the **gate**. Tick off all nine, one by one; whatever cannot be ticked goes into the remarks — not into the quotation.

| # | What to confirm | English phrasing you can copy directly | Why it must be settled first | What to do if they cannot answer |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Their role and decision power | "Before we go further, could you tell me your role — are you the one handling purchasing, or should I include a colleague?" | If you cannot find the person who can approve, nothing negotiated after that counts | Ask directly "Who else should I include?" and copy them in |
| 2 | Who is the Importer of Record | "Are you the importer of record, or do you buy through a trading company?" | The two-layer structure (importer + second-tier wholesaler) decides who you quote to and who their downstream is | Prepare on the trading-company basis first and ask them to fill in the customs broker information |
| 3 | Full specs (size / pattern / ply / load index) | "Let me quote against the sizes you actually run — mostly 12.00R20 for long haul, or 315/80R22.5 for city?" | Same money, different configuration = a different product, not comparable | Give three options to pick from; do not ask "what size do you want" |
| 4 | Quantity split by size | "May I ask how the total splits by size? We plan production per size." | Same total, sizes spread thin — production scheduling and freight allocation both get worse | Steer them: concentrating on 1–2 sizes is best for both of you |
| 5 | Destination port + trade terms | "Which discharge port — Jebel Ali, Dammam or Jeddah? And do you work on FOB or CIF?" | Different ports in the same country — the freight difference alone can eat your margin | Must be asked; never assume "the Middle East means Dubai" |
| 6 | Target price / landed-cost budget | "Do you have a target landed cost per unit? It helps me propose the right configuration rather than the cheapest one." | Only when you know their target band do you know which configuration to quote | If they will not give it, downgrade the question: "What price range do you sell at in your market?" — work backwards from their end price |
| 7 | Expected payment terms | "What payment terms do you normally work with your suppliers?" | Payment terms are bought with cash flow — you must first know how many days they want | Do not commit at this stage; only record it and keep it as a bargaining chip for the quotation |
| 8 | Current suppliers / brands in use | "Which brands are you running now, and what made you look for an alternative?" | Only when you know who they are unhappy with do you know which point to hit | Not answering is a common reaction; ask instead "What went wrong on your last shipment?" |
| 9 | Time pressure | "Any deadline — a certification date, a shipment window, or a delivery your customer needs?" | A requirement with no time pressure mostly will not close | A buyer with no deadline gets downgraded in priority; do not let them take up your main time |

Three hard gates: items 3, 4 and 5 (full specs / quantity split by size / destination port and trade terms) — if any one is missing, do not move into quoting. A quotation written that well is still waste paper — what is wrong is not the numbers, it is the premise.

Items 6, 7 and 8 are the **hidden three**: not being able to get them does not block quoting, but you must record the fact that "they would not say" itself in the remarks. A buyer who will not even say which brand they use is usually shopping for prices.

**Step 9: Lock the concession limits before quoting (wording aligned with `scripts/quote_engine.py`)**

This stage does not cut prices, but when it is your turn to cut, how far you can go must be thought through now. Think about it once you are in the quotation stage and your hand will shake — if you are not calm, the moment they press you, you give in; same as learning the rules at the card table mid-game. The project script `scripts/quote_engine.py` has already calculated the quotation, the three tiers of concession amounts and the floor price (fill in the parameters with your own costs):

```
python scripts/quote_engine.py --cost 28 --freight 6 --margin 15
```
> The numbers above are the usage example built into the script; they do not represent a recommended profit structure. Replace them with your own numbers.

The script's real wording (constants in `scripts/quote_engine.py:47-64`, ladder algorithm in `145-166`; the table below is the actual output for that set of example parameters):

| Tier | Cut this tier | Cumulative cut | FOB | CIF | Condition to trade for it |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Quotation (starting point) | — | — | 33.17 | 39.23 | — |
| Tier 1 | -2.5% | -2.5% | 32.34 | 38.40 | First order ≥ 1 container (20GP/40HQ) and accepts 30% deposit |
| Tier 2 | -1.5% | -4.0% | 31.84 | 37.90 | Upgrade from trial order to annual framework / volume more than doubles |
| Tier 3 | -1.0% | -5.0% | 31.51 | 37.57 | Sign 1–3 year exclusivity or sole agency (regional protection in exchange for a lower price) |
| Floor price (never shown outside) | — | -6.0% | 31.18 | 37.24 | Do not concede |

Five disciplines you can read from this table — all hard-coded in the script, not friendly reminders:

1. Every price cut must trade for something in return. The script comment says it verbatim: "a concession must trade for a condition... a concession without a condition = giving money away" (`scripts/quote_engine.py:35-38`). A concession with nothing in return tells them you still have room to give — and what you traded for must be written into the email, not just noted in your head. The Tier 1 condition even hard-codes "accepts 30% deposit"; unwilling to pay a deposit, they never reach this tier.
2. Cuts shrink each time; move one tier at a time. The three tiers shrink incrementally: -2.5% → -1.5% → -1.0%. That shape itself is a signal: the less you give as you go, the more they slowly understand where the floor is.
3. Only ever quote the cumulative cut to the outside. The script outputs both "this tier" and "cumulative" columns; what you tell the customer is always the cumulative (2.5% / 4.0% / 5.0%). Quote "this tier −1.5%" as "1.5% off the quotation" and you have given away 2.5% for nothing, with no goodwill banked.
4. Tier 3 ≠ the floor price; 1% is still held back in between. Three tiers add up to only −5.0%; the floor is −6.0% (`scripts/quote_engine.py:53-54`). That 1% is the "always keep one in hand" the script deliberately leaves you — after three tiers, the only things left to negotiate are non-price chips.
5. The CIF floor cannot be worked backwards from a discount. CIF must be divided by `(1 − insurance uplift × rate)`, because the insurance premium is calculated on 110% of the CIF value; the CIF floor is also recalculated from the FOB floor, not arrived at by discounting the CIF quotation (`scripts/quote_engine.py:121-143`). Every time you change trade terms or a set of cost parameters, re-run the script.

Three verbal disciplines for the floor price (the script only guards the numbers for you; you have to guard your own mouth):
- Generate the customer version with `--audience client`. The script strips out the whole block of cost basis, FOB/CIF floor prices and internal reminders (`scripts/quote_engine.py:202-205`). Note the script's default output is the internal view containing the floor price — confirm which version you are sending before you send it.
- Never say "this is already our lowest price". What that sentence really says is "a little more pressure would work", and you have blocked your own way back.
- When pushed into a corner, steer the topic to **configuration**, not price: "To go lower I'd have to change the scope — different ply rating, simpler packaging." You can negotiate a lower spec; you do not negotiate past the floor.

> Wording-conflict note: the ladder table in [06-quote_en.md](06-quote_en.md) uses a different set of percentages (L1 −2% running down to L4 −8%). By the script wording above, −8% is already below the floor (quotation × 94%). Where the two disagree, go by the result of running the script yourself (`--margin`, `--contingency`, `--validity` can all be changed), and state in the handover notes which tier you are on.

**Step 10: How to break three kinds of deadlock**

| What the customer says | What they are really saying | Never do this | Next move |
|---|---|---|---|
| Read but no reply / replies then goes quiet | You rank low on their to-do list, or they are waiting for you to cut the price on your own | Send "Did you see my message?" over and over; send a fourth after the third; slip a price cut into a follow-up | ① Switch channel (email → WhatsApp / phone, especially effective in the Middle East) ② Every follow-up must carry **one new thing** (a spec they have not seen, a container-loading calculation, a market update); if there is nothing new, do not send it ③ Cap the number of attempts at the "3 touches then stop" rhythm in `scripts/followup_plan.py` (e.g. T0 is D0 / D2 / D5; still no response after the 3rd touch means stop unconditionally and archive; **an explicit refusal means stop immediately**) ④ Give them a way out at the end: "I'll leave this with you — will keep you posted if anything changes on our side." |
| "Let me think about it" / "I'll get back to you" | There is a real obstacle they have not said out loud: price, configuration, internal approval, or comparing suppliers | Cut the price immediately to test the water; chase with an empty "So when will you decide?" | Turn the vagueness into an either/or: "Is it the price level, or is there a spec we haven't matched yet?" Hesitation almost always comes from one specific reason; asking it out saves three rounds of guessing. Then break the decision down smaller: quote only one size, send the spec sheet first, book a 15-minute video call. |
| "Too expensive" / "Your price is higher than others" | Could be a real comparison, could be a standard pressure line | Start arguing "our quality is better"; cut immediately; ask back "so what price do you want" | ① **Ask for the scope**: "May I ask what scope the other offer covers — same size, same pattern, same packaging, same payment terms?" Much of what looks "cheaper" is a bare price hitting your price with certificates included ② **Pull it back to terms**: price depends on quantity and payment terms; pull the conversation back to the nine questions in Step 8 ③ If they give a specific competitor price, run `python scripts/quote_engine.py ... --anchor <their price>` once: the script will tell you directly whether to make up the difference with payment terms / lead time / certification, or not to cut hard |

About "being pushed on price before you have even quoted": this is the most common sequence error. No quotation, no such thing as "expensive". The standard answer is:

> "Happy to work on the number — first I need to know what I'm pricing: sizes, quantity per size, destination port, and whether you work FOB or CIF. Give me those and I'll come back with something real."
> Key point: **answer a question with a question**. The moment you quote, the negotiation changes from "getting clear" to "comparing numbers" — you have already lost the first round.

**Step 11: Adjust the communication rhythm to the buyer's location**

The same logic, but a different market means a different way of speaking. The table below is about differences in communication rhythm — it is not for sticking labels on people from a country; **the specific public holidays, religious calendar and working-day arrangements are as per the actual local situation for that year**:

| Market | Communication rhythm | Taboos | One-line adjustment |
|---|---|---|---|
| Middle East GCC (Saudi Arabia, UAE, etc.) | Relationships first; small talk cannot be skipped; short decision chains — the owner may decide on the spot; WhatsApp is the real battlefield, email is mostly for the record | Pushing hard for a reply; directly rejecting a number they gave; flooding them on their rest days at your own pace | One extra greeting in the email, one voice message on WhatsApp — worth more than three chase emails in a row |
| Latin America | More relationship-led; slow replies but very steady once trust is built; Portuguese for Brazil, Spanish for Spanish-speaking countries — English only puts you at a clear disadvantage | Cold, purely price-led emails; ignoring long local holidays and time-zone differences; pushing for replies | Do not push. One useful reply a week apart beats one formulaic reply |
| Europe | Concise; terms and certification documents must be written out clearly; email-led, no insistence on instant messaging | Too much small talk; piled-up adjectives ("high quality"); starting to talk price before the certification is on the table | Fewer adjectives, more data: ply rating, load index, certification names, the standards used |

[04-outreach_en.md](04-outreach_en.md) already put "send in the other side's time zone" in its checklist; this stage follows the same rule: the reply window follows their morning, not your overtime hours.

### Closing the deal and handling objections (four closing questions + the four most common objections)

A new enquiry must be answered within 12 hours; past that, the customer goes to someone else. Answer their question first (one sentence), then ask for the three-piece set.

Do not ask "will you buy" to close — ask "how will you buy":
- **Trial-order close** (afraid of risk): "Start with a small trial order, mixed sizes, and you inspect the goods yourself. Shall I get the PI ready?"
- **Choice close**: "Model A or Model B? I'll make the PI for the one you pick."
- **Time-limited close** (only use real information): "This sailing's space closes this week; once you confirm, I'll lock the space for you."
- **Silence close**: 48 hours after sending the PI, do not push; on day three send only one line — "PI is waiting for your confirmation, I've held the space until tomorrow" — then shut up.

How to answer the four most common objections:
- **"Too expensive"**: first ask "Are you comparing the same spec?" — much of what looks "cheap" is a cut-down spec (ply / thickness not enough). Get them to send the competitor's specs, make a comparison table, and argue on "cost per kilometre".
- **"Want payment terms"**: new customers always get the standard payment terms. What you can offer is "after 2–3 smooth shipments, I'll help you apply for payment terms" — trade the future for the present.
- **"I already have a supplier"**: do not make them switch; make them add. "Most customers keep two suppliers — one to secure supply, one to compare prices. Let me be your second quote; comparing costs you nothing."
- **"Send a sample first"**: fine — sample fee + courier fee are on them, **deducted from the first order** — the sample is effectively free, and you lose nothing when they order.

In one sentence: every sentence must serve the close. Twenty sentences about the weather with none of the three-piece set in them = a wasted conversation.

## 3. Pass criteria

The hard standards for "this stage is done well":

- The other side's role in the purchasing decision is confirmed, as is who the Importer of Record is (whether through a trading company).
- **The three-piece set is complete**: specs specific (size / pattern / ply / load index) + quantity split by size + destination port and trade terms clear.
- The 40HQ loading plan works on both volume and weight, and the numbers have been checked against the forwarder.
- The requirement confirmation sheet has been sent and confirmed / corrected by the other side, with **no early quotation and no cost structure revealed throughout**.
- At least seven of the nine pre-quotation questions are settled; **the three hard gates (items 3/4/5) must all be complete** — one missing means no entry into quoting.
- The hidden three (target price range / expected payment terms / current suppliers) have been probed, or the "other side refused to answer" has been explicitly recorded with its reason.
- The concession limits have been run through `scripts/quote_engine.py` into concrete numbers: three tiers are **incrementally shrinking**, one tier at a time, only the cumulative cut is quoted outside, and conceding to the limit (−5%) still leaves 1% short of the floor (−6%).
- **The floor price has not appeared in any customer-visible content**: the customer version was generated with `--audience client`, and the cost basis and floor price have been stripped out by the script.
- Answers to the eight high-frequency questions (MOQ / samples / lead time / payment / certification / why more expensive than peers / can it be cheaper / send a sample first) are prepared, so there is no need to answer on the spot with AI.

## 4. AI prompts

**Prompt 1: Qualifying question sequence design**

```
You are a senior export sales negotiator for a Chinese tire manufacturer. I've just received the
first reply from a buyer. Design my questioning strategy for the next 2-3 message exchanges.

BUYER INFO:
- Country / company type / my guess of their role: {fill in}
- What they wrote: {paste their exact message}
- What we sell: {your product lines, e.g. TBR, sizes, patterns}

OUTPUT:
1. INTENT READ — what does this reply actually tell me? Write 3 bullets separating:
   (a) real signal (they revealed something specific)
   (b) missing signal (they dodged something — list what they avoided revealing)
   (c) risk flag (if any)
2. QUESTIONS TO ASK NOW — max 4 questions, ordered by priority. For each question give:
   - the exact English sentence to send
   - why this question, and what a GOOD answer reveals vs a BAD answer
   - whether to phrase it as open-ended or as multiple choice
3. WHAT NOT TO ASK YET — 2-3 things I should hold back because asking too early gives away leverage
   (e.g. price, MOQ negotiation, lead time commitments)
4. THE SMALL ASK — one low-cost next step I can offer that keeps momentum without discounting
   (options: send spec sheet, quote one size as a sample, propose a video call, ask for their
   target market segment)

RULES: Do not draft a price or any number. Do not assume facts I did not give you. If their reply
is vague, say so explicitly and design the question that forces specificity.
```

Note in Chinese (translated): the most useful output of this prompt is "missing signal" — what the other side avoided saying often tells you more than what they said. For example, if they keep not mentioning who their downstream is or how much volume they move, they are very likely shopping for prices or have not decided themselves.

**Prompt 2: Container loading and quantity feasibility check**

```
You are a logistics and costing analyst for tire export. Help me sanity-check whether a deal
is workable BEFORE I quote.

GIVEN:
- Product: {e.g. TBR truck tires}
- Sizes and per-tire dimensions/weight (I'll provide or you ask): {fill}
- Proposed loading: {e.g. 1x40HQ mixed 3 sizes, 480 pcs total}
- Destination: {port}, trade term: {FOB/CIF}

DO THIS:
1. Compute total volume and total weight. Compare against typical 40HQ usable volume and
   payload — IMPORTANT: state that usable volume and payload vary by carrier/vessel and must be
   confirmed with the forwarder; give the calculation, not a guarantee.
2. Flag whether the load is volumetrically constrained or weight-constrained, and say which
   spec dominates.
3. Show the mixed-loading advantage vs single-SKU loading, with numbers, if mixing helps.
4. Tell me what breaks: over-volume, over-weight, uneven axle-load distribution, or restacking risk.
5. List the 4 questions I must ask my factory/forwarder before confirming this loading plan.

OUTPUT: a compact working sheet with the arithmetic shown, plus a "DO NOT QUOTE YET UNTIL" list.
Do NOT invent container capacity figures as if they were official — always tag them as
"confirm with forwarder".
```

Note in Chinese (translated): this prompt translates "the customer says 500 pieces" into "whether that sentence is physically possible". Beginners flip here most easily — quoting a quantity that does not fit in the container, or goes overweight. And it marks every number as "to be confirmed with the forwarder", so it never gives you false confidence.

**Prompt 3: Requirement confirmation sheet generator**

```
You are an export sales coordinator. Turn the following conversation into a formal requirement
confirmation to send back to the buyer for approval before quotation.

CONVERSATION NOTES (messy, mine):
{paste your notes / chat history / the buyer's own words}

OUTPUT an email with:
- Subject line (short, clear, professional)
- Opening line: summarizing what we agreed
- A SPECIFICATION TABLE: Size | Tread Pattern | Ply | Load Index | Quantity | Unit Price basis
  (leave unit price as "to be quoted" — never fill it)
- Logistics line: container type & quantity, total pieces, destination port, trade term,
  pickup port, packaging/labeling requirement
- Validity note: "Specifications above are per our discussion; please confirm or correct before
  we proceed to quotation."
- Closing: one sentence asking them to confirm or correct, and stating the next step + my
  expected timeline

RULES:
1. ONLY include specs that were actually mentioned in my notes. Anything unmentioned must appear
   as a blank marked "TO CONFIRM" — do not fill gaps with typical industry values.
2. Do not state any price, discount, lead time, or payment term yet.
3. Where my notes are contradictory or ambiguous, flag it inline with [NEEDS CLARIFICATION:
   ...] instead of silently choosing one reading.
4. English. Under 200 words excluding the table.
```

Note in Chinese (translated): the heart of this prompt is rules 1 and 3 — anything uncertain is left blank or flagged, never letting the AI decide for you with "industry norms". The confirmation sheet you send back is your amulet against "I thought you meant a different size".

## 6. The traps beginners step into most

**Trap 1: Quoting the moment the customer asks for a price**
- What it looks like: they say "send me your best price" and you reply with a whole price list.
- Why: afraid of slighting the customer, of looking unprofessional.
- What to do instead: answer with "Happy to — first, so the number is right, could you confirm: sizes, quantity, destination port, FOB or CIF?" Answer a question with a question. Quoting always comes after the requirements are confirmed.

**Trap 2: Asking "what size do you want" and getting "all of them"**
- What it looks like: the customer replies "all sizes" and you do not know where to start.
- Why: the question is too open.
- What to do instead: give options. "Do you move mostly 12.00R20 for long haul, or 315/80R22.5 for city? Our top sellers in your market are these two." Multiple choice is far easier to answer than an open question — and people are willing to reply; they do not have to think up the sizes for you.

**Trap 3: Forgetting to confirm the destination port**
- What it looks like: the customer wants CIF, you never asked which port, and quoted on the default Jebel Ali — but they actually want Dammam.
- Why: assuming "Dubai is the default port for the Middle East".
- What to do instead: before a CIF quotation, the port must be asked. Different ports in the same country can differ hugely on freight, and the quotation must state `CIF Dammam port of discharge` clearly.

**Trap 4: Quoting on a standard container without confirming the quantity**
- What it looks like: the customer says "200 pieces to start", you quote on 200 pieces, and only later find the 200 are spread across 5 sizes and the factory says that combination is hard to schedule.
- Why: the quantity was never split down to size level.
- What to do instead: ask how the 200 pieces split across sizes. Spread across too many sizes, your production scheduling and price both get worse. Concentrating on 1–2 sizes is best for you — and a direction you can actively steer during the negotiation.

**Trap 5: No confirmation sheet, working from memory**
- What it looks like: you quote straight after the conversation, and the customer says "I thought you meant a different size".
- Why: the two sides remember it differently, and nothing was left in writing.
- What to do instead: repeat the requirements back and send them for confirmation before quoting. It is the cheapest insurance there is.

**Trap 6: Promising everything to close faster**
- What it looks like: they want a 45-day lead time, no talk of 100% prepayment, free samples — all three agreed on the spot, and after signing you find it cannot be done.
- Why: in a hurry for this order, conceding things that should not be conceded.
- What to do instead: in the qualifying stage, do not commit to lead times or payment terms; only confirm requirements. Lead time, payment and compensation are matters for [06-quote_en.md](06-quote_en.md) and [07-order-delivery.md](07-order-delivery_en.md). A concession must only happen after something has been traded for it.

**Trap 7: blurting out a number every time you are asked**
- What it looks like: the customer asks "What is the MOQ", "Are samples free", "How long to deliver", and you answer every one seriously — then find your own statements do not line up: samples promised free, then freight said to be collect in the next email.
- Why: treating "answering" as casual chat. Any number you give in the qualifying stage, the other side will treat as your formal condition — and pass it on.
- What to do instead: answer with the response cards in section 7, three sentences, then pull the topic straight back to requirements. Anything involving a quantity, always first ask back "how do you plan to split it across sizes".

**Trap 8: Treating the "this-tier cut" as "this much off the quotation"**
- What it looks like: the script's Tier 2 says "this tier −1.5%", and you really do cut 1.5% off the quotation; the customer works it out and it does not match the cumulative you quoted.
- Why: not looking at the cumulative column. The three tiers are **incrementally shrinking** (−2.5% / −1.5% / −1.0%), and the cumulative is the concession quoted outside (2.5% / 4.0% / 5.0%).
- What to do instead: only ever quote the cumulative cut, one tier at a time. Move on only after seeing the reaction; never state all three tiers at once.

**Trap 9: Working CIF backwards from "FOB quotation plus freight"**
- What it looks like: the customer switches from FOB to CIF mid-way, and you quote "FOB quotation + freight" as CIF. The quotation comes out low — short by exactly the insurance premium that was never counted.
- Why: CIF is not addition but division — divide by `(1 − insurance uplift × rate)`, because the premium is calculated on 110% of the CIF value. Likewise the CIF floor is recalculated from the FOB floor, not by discounting the CIF quotation (`scripts/quote_engine.py:121-143`).
- What to do instead: every time you change trade terms or a set of cost parameters, re-run the script. Let it calculate both the quotation and the floor; do not work them backwards by hand.

**Trap 10: One tone for every market**
- What it looks like: sending a warm Middle-East-style "Dear friend, how are you today" email to a European purchasing manager; sending a three-line cold quotation to a Middle East customer.
- Why: not realizing that differences in communication rhythm are themselves a trust cost.
- What to do instead: adjust tone, channel and send times using the Step 11 table first, then send. Getting it wrong will not lose the order on the spot, but you have already lost the chance of "them replying to your second sentence" in the first round.

### 7.1 Response cards for the eight high-frequency questions (Chinese–English对照, copy as written)

A beginner's real fear is not "not knowing the price" — it is "the customer asks one question and I do not know how to answer it". The eight cards below basically cover every question a customer will ask before a quotation. Store them in your playbook; do not improvise on the spot.

How to use them, in one sentence: answer in one sentence, then immediately ask a question back, throwing the ball back to the nine questions in Step 9. Answering without asking means you are being interviewed by the customer.

| # | What the customer asks | Why answer this way | English wording (copy as written) | Never say |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | What is the MOQ? | Ask their quantity out first before giving a number, or you quote low and get trapped, or quote high and scare them off | "Our standard MOQ is one full container per size mix — but it depends on how you'd like to combine sizes. What total are you planning per shipment?" | Blurt out a specific piece count |
| 2 | Can you send a sample first? | A sample is a tool for filtering real from fake, not a free gift | "Sure — we can arrange a sample for your evaluation. Sample is chargeable with the freight on your courier account; the cost is deductible from your first order." | "Samples are free" / "We'll cover the freight" (unless you have already decided to bear it) |
| 3 | How long is the lead time? | Do not commit to a specific number of days in the qualifying stage; only give the structure | "Standard production is around one production cycle after deposit, plus transit time to your port. I'll confirm the exact window once sizes and quantity are fixed." | A specific number of days or a specific date |
| 4 | Payment terms? | First feel out their expectations, then give a range | "For first orders we usually work with T/T: deposit before production, balance against B/L copy. What terms do you normally use with your suppliers?" | "Anything is negotiable" / "We can do credit" |
| 5 | Do you have certificates? | Give the specific list, but bounce the specific applicability back to the official wording | "We can supply the certificates commonly required for your market — please confirm the exact scheme you need and I'll send the current list for checking. **The specific requirements and validity are as per the target country's latest official regulations and the issuing authority.**" | "We can do any certificate" |
| 6 | Why are you more expensive than your peers? | Do not argue; compare configuration and scope instead | "It may look higher, and I'd rather check the comparison with you: same pattern, same ply, same packaging, same payment terms? Often the gap is in the scope, not the tire." | "Our quality is better" / starting to explain your cost structure |
| 7 | Can it be cheaper? | No quotation = nothing is "expensive". Trade conditions for a discount | "Happy to sharpen it — the number moves with quantity and payment terms. If you can confirm [ ] pcs and [ ]% advance, let me see what I can do." | Cut a percentage on the spot / "Let me go and apply for it" |
| 8 | Cut 3% and I'll order | The most famous fishing line; it must be traded for a condition on the spot | "Happy to work with that — to make 3% work on my side I'd need one of these: a 40HQ instead of a trial quantity, or 30% advance instead of balance against B/L. Which is easier for you?" | Answering "yes" directly |

> Card 8 is the most valuable card in the whole set. "Cut X% and I'll order" almost never comes true without a condition in exchange — once they have your low price, the most common next sentence is "OK, let me think about it".

### 7.2 Standard phrasing for trading a concession

```
中文：这个价格我可以去争取，但它挂在这三个条件上：[数量] / [预付比例] / [有效期]。三个里你能满足几个？
英文：I can work on that number, but it's tied to three things: the quantity, the advance %, and how long we hold the price. Which of those can we lock in?
```

This sentence is good because it does three things in one move: gives them a way out (it can be fought for), states the price (those three conditions), and throws the ball back (let them pick). People usually accept conditions they picked themselves more readily than ones you force on them.
