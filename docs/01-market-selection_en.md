# 01 · Market Selection

> English version of [01-market-selection.md](01-market-selection.md). Translated from the Chinese original; figures, dates, commands and file names match the original exactly.

This step has one job: lock in **one** first-stop market (only one market for the first stop), and write down three hard reasons for picking it.

In one sentence: a beginner competes not on how many markets they chase, but on how narrow the market is — and how fast it produces the first order.

> **Upstream / downstream**: see the "7-stage overview" in [README.md](../README.md) | zero-to-export basics in [00-getting-started.md](00-getting-started.md) | next step: [02-find-leads_en.md](02-find-leads_en.md) (Find Leads).

## 1. Why this step comes first

The most expensive mistake in exporting is not picking the wrong market — it is attacking five markets at the same time.

What happens when you attack five markets at once? You have to write five sets of outreach templates, look up five sets of certification requirements, remember five sets of time zones, and deal with five kinds of payment habits. After three months, every market is stuck at "a few buyers asked for prices" and not one has reached a deal.

Do the opposite — one market only: in a month you can accumulate 200 real leads, figure out who calls the shots in that country, what certificates imports need, where distributors buy from, and what prices are acceptable. In three months you can land the first order.

The first order is not just about money. It proves that your whole chain — product, quotation, contract, documents — actually runs end to end. Once it has run once, you have something solid to stand on when you copy it to a second market.

So step one is not "research ten countries". It is "cut nine".

## 2. Step by step

**Step 1: Build the candidate pool from data, not from gut feel (half a day)**

Just follow the clicks — no export experience needed first:

1. Open **UN Comtrade** (the UN commodity trade database; free, downloadable after registration).
2. In Commodity, enter the HS subheading for the tires you sell — passenger tires, TBR truck/bus tires and OTR off-road tires each map to different subheadings. **Use the destination country's customs authority's latest official classification**, and do not copy a code table off the internet.
3. Reporter = the country you want to look at; Partner = World; Trade Flow = **Import**; pick the **most recent 3 years**.
4. Pick Primary Value (import value, USD) as the indicator.
5. Sort by import value, highest first. The **top 20 countries go into the candidate pool**.

The table you pull out is the candidate pool — no need to dream up countries yourself. The few countries that pop into most people's heads usually end up outside this table. That is normal; guessing and looking at data are two different things.

Candidate pool illustration (**format only, not a recommended order** — pull the real ranking yourself with the 5 steps above):
Saudi Arabia / UAE / Iraq / Qatar / Kuwait / Egypt / Morocco / Algeria / Poland / Mexico / Brazil / Indonesia / Vietnam / Thailand

> Elimination rule: if a country shows no import records under your HS subheading for several years running, delete it from the candidate pool outright — it does not go on to scoring.

**Step 2: Get the data sources and costs straight first (30 minutes)**

The easiest place for a beginner to get stuck is "where do I actually check this number". The table below is ordered by "can I get it for free" — run through it before talking about scoring.

| Data source | What to check | Cost | How to get it |
|---|---|---|---|
| UN Comtrade (UN commodity trade database) | The target country's import value, source-country structure and three-year trend for a given HS subheading | Free (downloadable after registration; quota as published on the official site at the time) | Search `UN Comtrade` → open the database → Commodity = HS subheading → Reporter = target country → Partner = World → Flow = Import |
| ITC Trade Map (International Trade Centre) | A friendlier view of import value / source countries / growth — good for seeing "who is selling to it" | Basic functions free after registration; deep modules paid (as announced on the official site at the time) | Search `ITC Trade Map` → pick a country → pick the product HS subheading |
| Target country's customs / national statistics authority website | Import data in the country's own terms, tariff rates, certification registration entry points | Mostly free | Search `[country name] customs import statistics`, `[country name] national statistics bureau foreign trade` |
| Target country's tire / auto-parts importers association, chamber of commerce directory | Ready-made buyer lists (an early input for the Find Leads stage) | Mostly free; a few charge membership fees | Search `[country name] tyre importers association`, `[country name] chamber of commerce member directory` |
| Trade fair exhibitor directories | Active buyers who exhibit year after year; also useful for checking whether a business is still operating | Mostly free; a few require registration | Search `[country name] auto parts expo exhibitor directory` (use the actual fair name from its official site) |
| Google Trends | A **rough direction** for demand heating up — a lead only, never a conclusion | Free | Search the product keyword in English → set region to the target country → look at the multi-year trend |
| Paid customs data services (bill of lading / customs declaration data providers) | Import records down to the company name, and purchase cycles | Paid (some offer free sample data) | Search `customs import data [country name]`, compare free quota and price before deciding |

Bluntly put: do not touch paid data until you have run through every free source in this table. The one thing paid data does that nothing else can is the "down to the company name" layer — and that layer belongs to [02-find-leads_en.md](02-find-leads_en.md), not to this step.

**Step 3: Score on three dimensions (1–2 days)**

Score each candidate country 0–5 on three dimensions. Fix the weights in memory: Language 1×, Demand 1×, Barriers 2×. The reasons are below.

- **Language (weight 1×)**: can you independently read this country's business information?
  - 0–1: the official language is completely unreadable to you and English does not circulate in local B2B either → **demote straight to a second-stop candidate**
  - 3: some industry material is readable; email works in English
  - 5: business English circulates; association / government directories have English versions
  - Basis for the score: use the directories from Step 2 — **can you open one member / exhibitor list page you can actually read**.
- **Demand (weight 1×)**: do not look at "total market size". Only look at three columns that can be reproduced from a data source:
  ① the target country's import value for that HS subheading in the most recent year; ② the three-year trend direction; ③ whether it is highly import-dependent (whether domestic manufacturing is a tiny share of the source-country structure). **Every column must be reproducible on UN Comtrade or Trade Map**; a column you cannot reproduce gets written as `UNVERIFIED` and scores nothing.
- **Barriers (weight 2×)**: certification system, tariffs, whether a local agent is mandatory, how harsh the payment terms are.
  - 5: no mandatory certification + low tariffs + no local agent needed
  - 0: complex mandatory certification system + high tariffs + local agent mandatory + only harsh payment terms accepted
  - **Why it counts double**: barriers do not decide "can you sell", they decide "can you ship it out and get the money back". Most cases where a first order dies, die on barriers, not on demand.
  - Write only the system name (e.g. Saudi Arabia: the SASO certification scheme, SABER is the registration platform). **No clause numbers, no tariff figures** — always leave it as "per the latest official announcement".

**Elimination line**: any dimension below 2 means out (especially "barriers 0–1 and demand ≤ 2" — the classic case of getting in but never getting out).
Rank the survivors by weighted total and **take the top 3**.

**Step 4: Make the trade-off (30 minutes) — memorize these four criteria first**

Criterion 1: **The first order's goal is to "run the full process once", not to "take the biggest market".** The first sorting key is always "fastest to the first order", not demand size.

Criterion 2: **Big demand but a high certification bar → second stop.** The certification cycle, agent cost and sample cost for a high-bar market are all sunk costs you pay yourself before the first order has proven anything.

Criterion 3: **Small demand but orders right away → that is the first stop.**

So stop agonizing and hit the one that can produce an order now. Three reasons:

1. What the first order really produces is one end-to-end run: inquiry → quotation → contract → documents → payment → shipment → after-sales. The difficulty of that process has almost nothing to do with market size — running it once in a small market is the same complete difficulty.
2. In a high-bar market, the costs land **before** you even know whether the demand is real. That is the easiest money to waste.
3. After one successful run you have a real case, real buyer feedback and real document templates — the three things that get you from 0 to 1 when entering a big market. Without them you cannot even get into a big buyer's procurement system.

Accept the cost: a practice market has a low ceiling and possibly low order values. So do two things in parallel — write down the "second-stop candidates"; and once the first stop is validated, **start early** on the certification research cycle for the high-bar markets. Do not wait until you want in to start moving.

Criterion 4: this step cuts it down to "1 first stop + 2 second-stop candidates". Do not leave extra candidates on the table — they will only distract you later.

**Step 5: 15-minute availability check (turn "I think" into "I found it")**

Do each of the top 3 in turn, 15 minutes each, all with a search engine — no contacts, no questionnaires:

```
Search 1: [country name] tyre importers association
Search 2: [country name] chamber of commerce member directory
Search 3: [country name] auto parts expo exhibitor directory
```

**Pass line (all three must hold at the same time)**:

1. Open at least **1** directory / member page that browses normally, with **≥ 10 company names** you can count on the page;
2. Of those, **≥ 3** carry a website link, or a phone / email field;
3. The page is in a language you can read (an English version, or you have a reliable way to translate it).

Fail → demote straight to a second-stop candidate. **This step is more reliable than the score table**: scores are on paper; a directory is really there.

**Step 6: Write the three reasons (30 minutes)**

Each reason must be written in the four-part form "fact + source + capture date + to be verified". Missing any part means it was not written:

```
[Dimension] Factual claim | Source: <data source + definition> | Capture date: ____ | To be verified: ____
```

Format example (**the blanks are placeholders — replace them with what you actually found; do not copy them as-is**):

- [Language] The country's association directory has an English version; business communication works in English | Source: `<name of the directory page you opened in Step 5>` | Capture date: `____` | To be verified: `whether non-English (local language) communication material is needed`
- [Demand] The country's imports under HS `____` ranked `__` in the candidate pool for the most recent year; domestic manufacturing is a low share of the source-country structure (import-dependent) | Source: `UN Comtrade (Reporter=____ / Partner=World / Flow=Import / Year=____)` | Capture date: `____` | To be verified: `whether the subheading classification matches the official customs classification`
- [Channel] At least `__` public directory pages yield potential buyers, averaging `__` companies per page | Source: `<list the page names>` | Capture date: `____` | To be verified: `when the directories were last updated`

Words like these are banned: huge market, enormous potential, bright prospects, Belt and Road dividend. Writing them is the same as writing nothing.

**Step 7: Set a minimum validation target + write the exit rules first**

The target is a number, not a vision:

```
Within 30 days: __ real leads / __ outreach emails / __ replies / __ quotation opportunities
(Reference range [example]: 20 leads / 100 emails / 5 replies / 2 quotation opportunities — adjust up or down for your order value and product category)
```

**When you miss it, attribute in this order** (do not start by blaming the market):

1. **Replies far below expectation** → most likely a list or channel problem. Go back to [02-find-leads_en.md](02-find-leads_en.md) and switch the route (customs → trade fair → social media). **Change the approach first, not the market.**
2. **Replies come but nobody asks for a quote** → most likely a product-fit or qualification problem. Re-check the four items in the "barriers" column from Step 3 (certification system / tariffs / whether a local agent is needed / payment terms), and run through the three L1 checks in [03-due-diligence.md](03-due-diligence.md).
3. **Quotation opportunities exist but every deal collapses** → that is good news: the market is right, the problem is in quotation and negotiation. Go to [05-negotiation.md](05-negotiation.md).
4. **All four are 0 at the same time** → only then consider switching markets, and re-pull the candidate pool from Step 1.

## 3. Pass criteria

The hard bars for "this stage is done" (check them one by one, do not go by feel):

- The market has been cut to **1** (plus 2 second-stop candidates on record), backed by a verifiable three-dimension score (every Language / Demand / Barriers item has a citable basis, not a guess).
- The three-dimension score has been computed with the weights applied (Barriers 2×), and countries scoring below 2 on any dimension have been eliminated, not ignored.
- Every fact and every number is written in the four-part form "source + definition + capture date + to be verified"; no more than 3 leftover `UNVERIFIED` items remain, and each one says where to verify it.
- The top 3 have all been through the 15-minute availability check, and **the three pass lines (≥ 10 company names / ≥ 3 contact details / page readable) are backed by a screenshot or link** — not passed from memory.
- All three hard reasons contain **concrete facts** (data source names, search keywords, cluster or directory names) — no empty phrases like "huge potential".
- The trade-off conclusion follows the four criteria: it is explicit that "the one that produces an order fastest goes first", and the big high-bar market is listed as a second stop, not vaguely kept around.
- The 30-day minimum validation target is **quantified** (lead count / emails sent / replies / quotation opportunities), and the attribution order for missing it is written down.
- ≥ 3 public trade fair / association / chamber directory names and their pages have been recorded, as direct input for [02-find-leads_en.md](02-find-leads_en.md).

> One-line self-check: hand your conclusion to someone who knows nothing about this country — can they **reproduce** it from what you wrote? If not, the stage is not done.

## 4. AI prompts

**Prompt 1: Three-dimension market scoring**

```
You are an export market analyst specialized in B2B tire sales (China-based exporter).
Task: Score the following candidate markets for a NEW exporter with limited budget and no
existing relationships: {paste your country list}.

Score each country 0-5 on three dimensions:
1. Language accessibility (English usable in B2B trade? official/industry data readable?)
2. Demand size (import volume for tires HS40 subheadings; growth trend; import dependence)
3. Entry barrier (certification scheme, tariff rate, agent requirement, payment terms difficulty)

CRITICAL RULES:
- Do NOT invent numbers. If you do not have a verified figure, write "UNVERIFIED - needs Comtrade
  or official customs check".
- For certification requirements, state the scheme NAME (e.g. SASO/SABER; Saudi: SASO is the certification scheme, SABER is the platform) but never fabricate
  article numbers or clause text. Add: "confirm on the official portal".
- For tariffs, write "check current tariff schedule of the destination customs authority".

Output format: a markdown table with columns Country | Language | Demand | Barrier | Total |
One-line reason | Verification needed. Then a short "Top 3 recommendation" section with
3 bullet points each, max 40 words.
```

In plain words: make the AI play export market analyst, but with a hard order not to invent numbers — anything uncertain gets marked UNVERIFIED. The point is to let the AI build the skeleton and flag what needs verifying, then you go check the official sources.

**The manual verification loop you must run after the AI answers (3 steps, every output)**

1. **Pull out every UNVERIFIED line** and, using the `Verification needed` column in Prompt 1's output, re-calculate it on UN Comtrade / ITC Trade Map with the same definition (Reporter / HS subheading / year).
2. **Verify the certification and registration entry points**: keep only the scheme name in the certification column (e.g. Saudi Arabia SASO certification scheme + SABER registration platform), then confirm the current scope and required document list on that scheme's official portal, and note the official page name in your remarks. No clause numbers, no guessed tariff rates.
3. **Write the verification results back in**: what can be verified gets "source + capture date"; what cannot be verified gets **the whole reason deleted** — do not write "about" or "roughly". One second-hand conclusion left in the document will contaminate every decision after it.

**Prompt 2: Target-market buyer profiles**

```
Act as a senior sales director for a Chinese tire manufacturer (exporting to {country}).
Based on the top 3 buyer segments below, produce a buyer profile card for EACH segment.

Segments: (1) independent tire importers & distributors, (2) tire dealer chains / retail groups,
(3) fleet & mining OTR tire operators, (4) tire distributors serving garages.

For each segment output EXACTLY these fields:
- Who they are (role in the import chain, who signs, who influences)
- Typical order reality: container type (20GP/40HQ), rough loaded quantity range,
  and 2-3 common specs (e.g. 12.00R20, 315/80R22.5, 7.00R16) — present as ranges/plausible
  examples, NOT as market data
- What they care about most: price / payment terms / certification / warranty claim handling /
  delivery reliability (rank top 3)
- Where they are findable: named PUBLIC sources only (customs data platforms, LinkedIn,
  trade fair exhibitor lists, chamber of commerce directories)
- The ONE question that makes them reply to a cold email
- Red flags that mean "not a real buyer" (e.g. asks for free sample of expensive goods,
  refuses video call, email domain mismatch)

Format as a markdown card per segment. Max 120 words per field. No marketing language.
```

In plain words: this splits "one big market" into four different kinds of buyers. Different buyers care about completely different things. A distributor looks at margin; a mining operator looks at wear life and claims handling. Split them apart and your outreach stops being one template for everyone.

**Prompt 3: Entry-path health check**

```
You are a trade compliance advisor. I am a Chinese exporter of tires planning to sell into
{country}. List the practical ENTRY CHECKLIST I must complete before the first shipment.

Cover, in order:
1. Product certification schemes that apply (name them; do NOT quote clause numbers)
2. Labeling / marking requirements (language, sidewall, invoice)
3. Customs HS classification note (tire categories: passenger, truck/bus, TBR, OTR, inner tube)
4. Typical importer-of-record arrangements and whether a local agent is expected
5. Payment terms that first-time buyers usually get (T/T, L/C at sight, CAD, O/A risk)
6. 3 things I should confirm with a local customs broker BEFORE sending samples

Rules: every item must end with a "VERIFY AT:" line naming the official authority or portal
to check. If uncertain, say so explicitly. Never invent duty rates or document numbers.
```

Put simply: this one exists to stop the "samples already shipped, then I find out the certification fails" scenario. The core is that every item must say where to verify it — treat the AI as a lead generator, not an answer machine.

## 6. Common beginner traps

**Trap 1: Picking the US / EU first (the most frequent beginner mistake)**

If you are about to write the US or EU as your first stop, read this first, then decide whether to continue:

- What it looks like: the macro data is the biggest and easiest to find, everyone around you talks about it, it looks the most "proper" — so the first stop gets locked to the US or EU.
- Three ways it is fatal for a beginner:
  1. **Certification comes first**: both places have mature, frequently updated tire market-access systems (US-side federal requirements such as DOT, EU-side E-marking and tire labeling requirements). The system names can be looked up, but **the exact scope and list must be verified on the official portals**; this kind of compliance work takes a long cycle and needs a local responsible party.
  2. **Buyers already have their queues filled**: big buyers have stable supplier lists; what a newcomer gets is usually the hardest batch to win. You are facing "supplier-switching cost", not "price level".
  3. **Heavy liability**: product liability, recalls and claims handling are hard constraints there. Trying to carry that whole set on your first order, you can easily lose more than you make.
- The right move: put the US / EU (and any market that is "big GDP + complex certification system") **into the second-stop candidates**, then, starting from the criteria in Step 4, pick a market where you can touch 20 real leads within 30 days.
- **Not "never do it" — sequence it**: do it after you already have a first order, real cases and document templates. The success rate differs by an order of magnitude.
- Remember the order: proving you can deliver > market size.

(The same trap has a variant: taking Brazil, India or another market with complex tariffs and local rules as the first stop. Same problem, same nature.)

**Trap 2: Treating the finished score table as the finished selection**
- What it looks like: the table is done, a first place is marked, and you go off to write outreach emails.
- Why: the score table is a paper judgment that has never touched reality.
- The right move: **Step 5's 15-minute availability check is mandatory**, not optional. Spend 15 minutes per top-3 country actually counting 10 company names and 3 contact details in a directory. Whether you can find them beats how accurate the score is — a country you cannot find is a paper market no matter how high it scores.

**Trap 3: Using a three-year-old report as the current state**
- What it looks like: quoting a 2021 article saying demand in some country is exploding, and writing the conclusion straight into the plan.
- Why: search engines still rank old articles first; new data is less visible.
- The right move: mark every number with its source + publication time. Data older than a year must be re-verified. Customs data, UN Comtrade and the target country's official statistics are hard-number sources; industry articles are only leads.

**Trap 4: Giving certification requirements a "heard about it" pass**
- What it looks like: hearing that the Middle East needs certification, and never digging further, because nothing has shipped yet.
- Why: certification is a "must finish before shipping" item, and since it does not affect today's orders, it keeps getting pushed back.
- The right move: the day after picking the market, look up the target country's certification system and registration entry point, and write "which documents, who handles it, roughly how long" in three lines stuck on your desk.

**Trap 5: Using "my friend is there" as a reason**
- What it looks like: deciding to focus on a country because you know one local customer.
- Why: a friend can open a door, but not the whole door — market capacity and competition are still blank.
- The right move: keep the friend as a separate "warm-start channel"; market selection still follows the three-dimension score. Walk on two legs; do not treat one leg as the whole thing.

**Trap 6: Buying paid data before the free sources are exhausted**
- What it looks like: hearing about a customs data platform that can find company names, paying immediately, and discovering a month later that the free sources already answered 80% of the questions.
- Why: treating "more data" as "better decisions" and ignoring that this step only needs a ranking.
- The right move: run through every free source in the Step 2 table before evaluating paid data. **Paid data is ammunition for which stage? [02-find-leads_en.md](02-find-leads_en.md) — not market selection.**

**Trap 7: Switching approaches over and over for the wrong market (attribution error)**
- What it looks like: no replies in week one, so you change the email template; still nothing in week two, so you change channels; week three you change keywords — three months of changing posture, never looking back at the market itself.
- Why: confusing "wrong market" with "wrong approach", so you keep patching the surface forever.
- The right move: follow the 4-step attribution order in Step 7. **Replies that do not close = the market is right, do not touch the market**; only re-pull the candidate pool when all four are 0 at the same time. The attribution order is written down precisely to stop panicked thrashing.
