# 03 · Due Diligence

> English version of [03-due-diligence.md](03-due-diligence.md). Translated from the Chinese original; figures, dates, commands and file names match the original exactly.

> Due diligence, in one sentence: grade every lead A/B/C, throw out the shell companies and scammers early, and put your best hours on buyers who can actually close.
>
> The logic is plain — one outreach email costs nothing, but three months wasted on a fake customer is expensive. Screen first, then pitch. It is the only way to save money in exporting.
>
> Due diligence answers only two things: ① is this company worth your time; ② what is the first sentence (one quotable piece of evidence, written back into the lead table's `note` column, which becomes the first line of the outreach email in the outreach stage).
>
> If you cannot answer those two, what you wrote is not due diligence — it is an essay nobody will read.

## 1. Why this step comes first

The mistake beginners love most: treating "someone replied to my email" as "I have a customer".

What actually costs you in real life is not silence — it is:
- You spend two weeks preparing a quotation, costing it out, sending a price — and the other side was never a buyer at all, just a competitor fishing for your prices
- You send three follow-ups with detailed specs, they take everything and vanish without even a "not a fit"
- You ship samples at their request, the parcel is already on its way, and only then you realize you never checked whether their company is even real

Add those up and they roughly decide whether you survive the first six months.

The core of due diligence is one line: spend the least time throwing out the obviously unreliable ones first. A-grade companies make you money; C-grade companies bleed you dry — and a C's flaws usually show in two minutes.

## 2. Compliance red lines (read this first)

Only use public sources you can open fair and square.

Allowed:

- Company official websites (company information, product catalogs, contact details, founding statements)
- Public customs / import-export data (UN Comtrade, official statistics bodies of each country, the public search functions of licensed commercial data platforms)
- LinkedIn public company pages (roles, company information, public posts)
- Public trade fair exhibitor directories and industry association membership directories
- Press releases published through a company's official channels, and official social media accounts
- Public company registration information (public searches of the target country's official business registry)
- Search engines and public web page caches
- Company material the customer sends you on their own initiative

Do not touch these:

- Paid "customer lists" or "packaged customs data" of unknown origin — data from unknown sources often comes from leaked databases or resale chains, and using it carries legal liability
- Any service claiming it can "crack customs data" or "export customer email addresses"
- Impersonating the other side to extract information (pretending to be a peer, pretending to be a customer, pretending to be a courier)
- Obtaining, buying, or stitching together personal information without consent (private phone numbers, private emails, ID document details)
- Bribing insiders or the other company's staff to get customer lists and price systems
- Scraping or mirroring website content in violation of a platform's terms of service
- Using stolen databases or leaked contact lists

Criminal-law red lines (a floor, not a suggestion):

Never do any of the following, no matter how badly you want the deal. They are not "grey-area tactics" — they are the explicit target of Article 253-1 of the Criminal Law of the People's Republic of China, the crime of infringing citizens' personal information: selling or providing citizens' personal information to others, or stealing or otherwise illegally obtaining it, in violation of state regulations, is subject to criminal liability where the circumstances are serious (exact sentencing follows the current article and judicial interpretations):

- Social engineering databases (社工库), credential-stuffing data, and any "type in a phone number and get the person" lookup service
- Hotel check-in records, accommodation records, travel records, call records, **location tracks**
- **Bank statements**, credit reports, social security / housing fund records
- **Getting someone's number through a replacement SIM card (补卡查号)**, or pulling someone's information through contacts inside a telecom operator or courier company
- Buying off or directing the other company's staff (an "insider", 内鬼) to leak customer lists, price systems, contracts
- Doxxing (人肉搜索), digging into the other person's family and private social accounts, stitching fragments of private information into a dossier

Draw the line here: what you check is "the company's public business information", not "one person's private life". The moment the target slides from a company to a specific person, you have already crossed it.

The principle is simple too: only a web page you can openly open counts. If a piece of information takes "special means" to obtain, do not use it and do not write it into the file.

Going to prison is not a line item in an export ROI calculation — do not get this sum backwards.

Due diligence conclusions are for internal decisions. They are not for judging, reporting, or spreading information about other people.

## 3. Due diligence answers only two questions

Nail the purpose down first — only then does everything after it have a boundary:

1. **Is this company worth your time?** → Produces an A/B/C rating (definitions in this page's rating table; the mapping to outreach tiers is in [04 · Outreach](04-outreach_en.md)).
2. **Where does the first sentence come from?** → Produces one piece of evidence that "only holds for this company", written back into the lead table's `note` column, which becomes the why-them line of the outreach email.

Due diligence is not responsible for three things:

- It does not produce a complete report. You cannot check everything, and you do not need to — a customer file is built up through follow-up, not through research.
- It does not judge whether the other side is "good or a scammer". It only records signals and the action taken — no moral judgments, nothing passed on outside.
- It does not check any natural person's private information (see the criminal-law red lines in Section 2).

There is exactly one test for whether due diligence is done: can you say in one sentence "why this company, and where the first sentence comes from". If you cannot, it is not done yet.

## 4. Seven-dimension checklist: where to check / what counts as a red flag

Check in this order. The first three decide "is it a real company and a real buyer"; the last four decide "is it worth pitching, and how do I open".

| # | Dimension | Where to check (public sources) | What counts as a red flag |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Identity and track record**: does the company really exist? Does the registered name match the name it uses? | Public search of the target country's official business registry (e.g. UK Companies House, the Hong Kong Companies Registry search portal, the target country's industry/commerce authority public portal); the About / Imprint / footer company information on the official site; search the company's full name | No registered entity can be found at all; the registered name does not match the name it uses, with no explanation; founded less than a year ago but claims "decades of history" |
| 2 | **Size and scale**: how many people, how many locations, how big is the operation? | About / store map / product catalog on the official site; the employee-count range shown on the LinkedIn company page; trade fair exhibitor directories; industry association membership directories | The official site claims "200+ outlets" but LinkedIn shows single-digit headcount; the store map is all blank or placeholder; the size figures come from the official site alone, with nowhere to cross-check |
| 3 | **Channel role**: importer / tier-1 wholesaler / tier-2 wholesaler / retailer / fleet buying for its own use? | The company's own description using distributor / wholesale / dealer / import and similar words; the number of brands in the product catalog (many brands = distributor, a single own brand = a peer); industry association membership directories | Calls itself a distributor but shows no evidence of any downstream channel; is itself a peer brand manufacturer (price-fishing risk — do not give a quotation sheet); the role does not match your price band (pitching fleet prices to an importer is a wasted conversation) |
| 4 | **Import footprint**: is it really importing? Does the product category match yours? | UN Comtrade (comtradeplus.un.org); official statistics / customs public searches of each country; the public search functions of licensed commercial data platforms; public tender announcements on the target country's government procurement portals | Zero import records; the imported categories have nothing to do with your product; only a stray order or two over several years (a record exists but no scale); only exports, no imports |
| 5 | **Credit and risk signals**: lawsuits, enforcement, on a sanctions list? | Public court / judicial searches of the target country; public corporate-credit disclosure platforms; Google News searches for "company name + lawsuit / bankruptcy / fraud"; OFAC / EU / UN public sanctions list searches | Enforcement, judgment-defaulter or bankruptcy/liquidation records → downgrade, and never sell on credit; **on a sanctions list → stop immediately: no contact, no quotation, no samples** |
| 6 | **Who is the key person**: who can sign off? How do you reach them? | The People tab on the LinkedIn company page (look for purchasing / procurement / sourcing roles); the Team / Contact page on the official site; trade fair speaker and guest lists; the company's official social media accounts | No real employees on LinkedIn, or only the boss; no purchasing / supply-chain role at all; the only contact is info@ or a free mailbox |
| 7 | **What has it been doing lately**: anything to open with? | Update dates on the official site's News / Products pages; LinkedIn company posts; official social media accounts; the years it exhibited at trade fairs in the last two years; recent tender announcements | The official site's last update was years ago; recent posts are all hiring and motivational quotes, no business activity; no new products, exhibitions, or tenders in the last two years |

One more check that takes 30 seconds: **the email domain**. A free mailbox (gmail / yahoo / hotmail / outlook / qq / 163 and similar) as the only contact method → red flag; an email domain that does not match the official site's domain → red flag.

> One rule across all seven dimensions: if you cannot find it, write "not found" — do not write "does not exist". Not found is not the same as does not exist. A downgrade has to say which item was not found, or the next re-check steps on the same pit again.

## 5. Step by step (three-level funnel + time boxes)

Time boxes (fixed in writing — leave when the time is up. This is where beginners most easily dig without end):

| Level | Cap per lead | Who does it | Action when time is up |
|---|---|---|---|
| L1 screening | **2 minutes** | Every new lead | Fails → grade C, write one line of reason, move on |
| L2 standard | **10 minutes** | Leads that passed L1 | Produces A/B/C + one `note` line |
| L3 deep dive | **30 minutes** (hard cap 45) | **A-grade only** | Produces a 5-line profile, then straight into outreach |
| **Total per company** | **45 minutes hard cap** | — | Still not done when time is up = not enough information; grade on the evidence you have, fill in the rest during follow-up |

> The funnel checks the same seven dimensions above, just at different depths: L1 scans the surface, L2 cross-checks, L3 adds depth. Do not invent your own system outside the funnel — if you are still researching at 3 AM, you are mostly inventing new ways to check. 45 minutes of due diligence is enough; past that you are not doing due diligence, you are stalking.

**L1 screening: 2 minutes per lead — throw out the obviously unreliable ones first**

Only four questions; any one you cannot answer means a provisional C grade: (one standard for the whole project: L1 is four questions. If any other quick-reference doc writes it as three, the four here win.)

1. **Is the website alive?** The homepage loads and shows recent update traces (news, press releases, product pages). Will not load, only a Coming soon page, or stuck in 2019 → downgrade straight away
2. **Is there an address and a contact method?** A physical address + a domain email (not just gmail). Only one WhatsApp number → downgrade but do not kill it (normal for small countries)
3. **Does it have wholesale / trading attributes?** The site uses distributor, wholesale, trading, import, dealer, supply or similar words, or the product catalog covers many brands
4. **Are there any signs of scale?** Headcount, store count, founding year, coverage area. No sign of any of them → be cautious

L1 output: a lead not worth checking further gets graded C on the spot, with the reason written down, then move to the next one. How many you cut has no standard answer — it depends on your list quality. Do not let C-grade leads in just to hit a number.

**L2 standard: 10 minutes per lead — judge "is it worth investing in"**

Four cross-checks for what is left:

1. **Cross-check against customs / trade data**: does this company have real import records? Does the imported category match yours? Is the frequency reasonable? (An old customer importing every year = a good signal; one order in three years = be cautious)
2. **Cross-check the official site's self-description**: does what the site says it is contradict what the trade data shows it does? A company calling itself "the GCC's largest tire distributor" with trade records showing 2 containers imported a year is a contradiction
3. **Contact details consistency**: do the email domain, the company site name, and the LinkedIn company name match? If not, ask why
4. **People-level information**: how many people are on LinkedIn, and is there a purchasing role? Is the company "one boss" or "a team"? (One reminder: a CEO title on LinkedIn is not necessarily a real CEO — but it definitely wants you to think he is. Treat titles as a reference, not as evidence.)

L2 output: split into A/B/C.

**Rating standard (copy and use as-is):**

| Rating | Test | Handling |
|---|---|---|
| **A** | Passes all of L1 + has import records + category matches + a specific buyer found | First touch within 48 hours, invest people |
| **B** | Passes all of L1 + category roughly matches + a contact can be found but purchasing evidence is weak | Into the T1/T2 sequence, try low-cost approaches first |
| **C** | Any L1 item fails, or contact details are incomplete, or the information contradicts itself | Do not invest people; only low-frequency content nurturing |

**L3 deep dive: A-grade only, 30 minutes (hard cap 45)**

Only A-grade is worth a deep dive, four actions:

1. **Scale profile**: roughly how much it imports, what channel types it probably covers (who it wholesales to, retail or fleets)
2. **Decision-chain judgment**: owner buys direct / purchasing manager / a trading company buys on its behalf (the Middle East's common two-tier structure: importer + tier-2 wholesaler)
3. **Current opening**: whose goods is it selling now? Which brands does the official site show? Which new product or model is it pushing? — this decides the angle of your outreach email
4. **Risk inventory**: founded very recently but asking for a large credit line, only accepts FCA/EXW and refuses any prepayment, address is a mailbox / virtual office, the company name leaves no other trace in search at all

L3 output: a 5-line profile that directly decides how the outreach email is written. Line 5, "outreach email angle", is mandatory — if that line is empty, the L3 was wasted.

---

Write-back: put the conclusions into the lead table (skip this step and everything before it was wasted)

Due diligence output has to land in these columns of the CSV, or it is just a memory that will be forgotten:

| Lead table column | What to write | Who reads it |
|---|---|---|
| `note` | One piece of evidence you **can say to their face**, written in English, e.g. `Importer record: 6 shipments/year from Asia, 40HQ container` | `lead_score.py` scans it for the "purchasing signal" score; `outreach_gen.py` pastes it **verbatim into the outreach email** as the why-them sentence |
| `contact` | The key person's role found in due diligence (purchasing manager / buyer / owner) | `lead_score.py` counts it for keyword hits; `outreach_gen.py` uses it as the salutation |
| `source` | Switch to the most credible source after due diligence (`customs` > `trade_show` > `linkedin` > `search`) | `lead_score.py`'s "source credibility" is worth 30 points, second only to contact details |
| `years` | Years since registration / founding (leave blank if not found — do not guess) | Maturity score |

Two rules for writing `note`:

1. **Only write things you can say in public.** `outreach_gen.py` pastes `note` verbatim into the English email ("your note mentioned: …"), so internal judgments ("looks like a shell company", "probably a peer fishing for prices") must **never go in this column** — save them in a `risk` column of your own, or an internal notes area.
2. **Use factual words, do not stack keywords to farm points.** Words like `rfq / quotation / container / 40HQ / purchasing manager` trigger purchasing-signal bonus points, but only write them when you genuinely have the evidence; invented keywords only make you misjudge your own priorities.

After writing back, run it once and see whether the grades changed:

```bash
python scripts/outreach_gen.py my_leads.csv --tier T0
```

## 6. Red-flag list and what to do about each

When any signal below appears, do what the "Action" column says — do not improvise on the spot:

| Signal | Action |
|---|---|
| Only contact method is a free mailbox | Downgrade to B; get a domain mailbox first, then come back |
| Email domain does not match the official site's domain | Downgrade; first ask what the entity relationship is (subsidiary / brand name?) |
| Official site not updated for years / only a Coming soon page / obvious template-site copy | Downgrade to C |
| Only one WhatsApp number, no address, no official site | Downgrade to C (small countries can stay in the watch pool, but **never give any credit terms**) |
| Company name does not match the registered name | Pause; verify identity first, ask the other side to clarify the entity in writing |
| No real employees on LinkedIn, no purchasing role | Downgrade to B |
| Enforcement / judgment-defaulter / bankruptcy records | Downgrade to C, and **only do prepayment or sight L/C** |
| **On a sanctions list** | **Stop immediately**: no contact, no quotation, no samples |
| Zero import records but claims to be a big distributor | Downgrade to B/C; treat as "company is real but commercial evidence is thin" |
| Is itself a peer brand manufacturer | Move to the peer-handling process, **do not give a quotation sheet** |
| First contact asks for a large credit line / only accepts EXW-FCA and refuses any prepayment | Downgrade; only prepayment or sight L/C |
| Address is a mailbox address / virtual office | Downgrade; ask for a verifiable real address before talking samples |
| Asks for payment to a third-party account that does not match the contract entity | **Stop directly** — this is the most classic trade-fraud pattern, not a negotiation issue |

## 7. Pass criteria

The hard bars for "this stage is done well":

- All information sources are **public and legal** — no paid blacklist data, no grey channels, no impersonation to extract information.
- L1's four questions each have a recorded judgment with traces; L2 is done, and every contradiction between import records and the official site's self-description has been handled one by one (downgraded or removed).
- Every lead carries an **A/B/C rating** with its reason (definitions in this stage's rating table).
- A-grade leads have a finished L3 deep profile (decision-chain judgment + opening angle), ready to enter outreach within 48 hours.
- The red-flag list is built, and sensitive personal information is only used for internal sales decisions, never passed on.
- **The time box held**: no more than 45 minutes per company in total, L3 only spent on A-grade (overrunning = this stage was not done well).
- **Written back**: every A/B lead's `note` carries one quotable piece of evidence, `source` updated to the most credible source after due diligence; internal risk judgments are not written in `note`.
- Every lead has its action decided (enter outreach / downgrade / stop) — not just a letter filled in.
- Every due diligence conclusion carries an evidence grade: [hard data] first-hand sources such as official site text, registries, customs data; [verbatim quote] a person's / media's own words, with the source noted; [inference] a reasonable inference from evidence, which must be labelled "inference". What cannot be found is written "to be verified" — **never invent**.

## 8. AI prompts

**Prompt 1: Quick due diligence on one customer (L1/L2)**

```
You are a due-diligence analyst for B2B export sales (Chinese tire manufacturer). Using ONLY the
public information I paste below, assess this company. Strictly no outside guesses.

INPUT DATA (company name, country, website text, customs/import records, LinkedIn snippets):

OUTPUT in this exact format — keep each section under 80 words:

1. IDENTITY
   Legal name / trading name / whether they are importer, distributor, retailer, or garage chain
   (quote evidence for each claim)

2. PHYSICAL PRESENCE
   Address found / address type (own office, workshop, PO box, virtual office) / branch count
   Say "NOT STATED" if absent.

3. BUSINESS SIGNALS
   Product range overlap with {TBR / OTR / PCR tires} / brands they carry / wholesale terms
   mentioned / years active / size signals (employees, locations, annual turnover if disclosed)

4. TRADE BEHAVIOUR CROSS-CHECK
   Compare what the company CLAIMS about itself with what the import records SHOW.
   Explicitly flag every contradiction. If no import records were provided, write
   "NO TRADE DATA PROVIDED — cannot verify".

5. CONTACTABILITY
   Named person? / role? / verified-looking email? / phone/WhatsApp? / LinkedIn profile?
   If only a generic mailbox exists, say so.

6. RED FLAGS (list as bullets, each one sentence)
   Examples to check: domain age unknown but company claims decades of history; only gmail contact;
   address not verifiable; asks for credit terms on first contact; brand claims impossible for
   their size; website content looks AI-generated boilerplate; company name barely appears
   anywhere else on the web.

7. VERDICT
   Grade A / B / C with ONE sentence reason and ONE concrete next action.

RULES: Use only what I provided. Never invent an email, phone, address, or figure. If a fact is
absent, "NOT STATED" is the correct answer and more useful to me than a plausible guess.
All information here will be used for internal sales prioritization, so accuracy matters more
than completeness.
```

Note in Chinese (translated): the heart of this prompt is sections 4 and 6 — forcing the AI to put "what the other side says about itself" and "what its actual trade behaviour shows" side by side and collide them, then list the red flags. Scammers and small shell companies basically give themselves away in those two places.

**Prompt 2: Deep profile for an A-grade customer (L3)**

```
Act as a Key Account Manager preparing an account plan BEFORE the first outreach.

Company: {name}, {country}. All verified facts I collected: {paste your verified notes}

Produce a strict one-page account plan, in this order:

SECTION 1 — SNAPSHOT
Table: Legal entity | Type (importer/distributor/retailer/fleet) | Est. import scale |
Likely decision-maker role | Our product fit (high/medium/low) | One-line strategic note

SECTION 2 — DECISION CHAIN
Map the likely buying chain for this market: who signs the contract, who specifies specs,
who pays, who receives goods. For GCC-type markets, explicitly state whether a two-tier
structure (importer + secondary wholesaler) is likely. Name the ROLES I should target, not
invented person names.

SECTION 3 — CURRENT SUPPLY SITUATION
Only using provided facts + clearly-labelled assumptions: which brands they likely carry,
what spec categories they must move, what a new supplier must beat on (price? lead time?
warranty? documentation?).

SECTION 4 — OPENING ANGLES (max 3)
Each angle: one sentence hook + the single piece of evidence that makes it credible + the
question I should ask them to make them reply.

SECTION 5 — DEAL KILLERS
3 reasons this deal could still die, and the earliest signal that would tell me to stop.

RULES: Every claim must be tagged [VERIFIED] (I gave you the fact) or [ASSUMPTION] (your
inference). No unlabelled statements. Do not invent people, certifications, tariffs or numbers.
```

Note in Chinese (translated): the point is the [VERIFIED] / [ASSUMPTION] tags — forcing the AI to separate "the facts you gave it" from "its own inferences", so you can see at a glance which line you still need to verify yourself. Section 4's "hook + evidence + question" can go straight into the first line of the outreach email.

**Prompt 3: Batch grading and quota allocation**

```
You are sales operations planning. Below are 60 leads with their L1/L2 verification results.
I have limited capacity: roughly 8 hours of research and outreach per week.

Allocate my time, not just grade them.

Deliver:
1. Grade A / B / C counts and total estimated hours to complete L3 on each A
2. A 4-week calendar: Week 1-2 = L3 on which A accounts and why these first (rank by
   "expected value per hour" = (deal size × probability) / hours spent)
3. Which accounts to deliberately DROP despite looking attractive, with the reason
   (bad signal, too slow, requires credit risk you can't carry, etc.)
4. A red-flag watchlist: accounts that look fine on the surface but have one worrying signal —
   flag with the exact signal and what to verify next time we talk

Rules: Use only the verification results I provided. If two accounts are tied, prefer the one
with clearer verified evidence over the one with bigger-sounding size claims.
```

Note in Chinese (translated): this one turns "grading" into "resource allocation". The classic beginner mistake is doing L3 on every A and finishing none of them properly. Real trade-offs mean calculating the expected value per hour and deliberately dropping a few attractive-looking ones.

## 10. Common beginner traps

**Trap 1: Outreach first, no due diligence**
- What it looks like: 200 names in hand, the 50 biggest-looking ones all emailed on the same day.
- Why: due diligence feels like a waste of time — "send it out and see who replies" feels faster.
- The right move: at minimum run the first two L1 questions (is the website alive, does it have trading attributes). 2 minutes per lead, 200 leads is about an hour. The money for samples costs a hundred times more.

**Trap 2: Buying a "customs data customer list"**
- What it looks like: spending a few hundred on a pack of "Middle East tire customer lists" — two thousand email addresses, looking like a great deal.
- Why: the data is of unknown origin — much of it leaked from databases, or email formats simply made up.
- The right move: only use the public search functions of trade data platforms and public sources. Data of unknown origin = legal risk + high bounce rate + a damaged domain reputation.

**Trap 3: Only checking "does this company exist", not "is it a buyer"**
- What it looks like: the company is confirmed real, so it gets an A.
- Why: confusing "the company exists" with "the company really trades". Many companies genuinely exist but never import, or do not deal in your kind of product.
- The right move: existing is only the entry ticket — import records / trade behaviour are what decide the rating. Real but zero trade signals → B at most.

**Trap 4: Believing the official site's self-promotion**
- What it looks like: the site says "leading distributor in GCC with 200+ outlets", and it gets marked A on the spot.
- Why: anyone can write that sentence, and it may be template-site output.
- The right move: every self-promoted number gets labelled "company's own claim", and cross-checked against third-party data (customs records, trade fair exhibition scale, LinkedIn headcount). If it does not match, downgrade — no need to call them out.

**Trap 5: Turning due diligence into academic research**
- What it looks like: 40 minutes on one lead, still researching at midnight, no outreach done.
- Why: mistaking "thorough" for "correct" and chasing exhaustive detail.
- The right move: follow the Section 5 time-box table (L1 two minutes, L2 ten minutes, L3 thirty minutes, 45 minutes hard cap per company). Leave when time is up. An A-grade customer's value shows in the follow-up, not in the due diligence.

**Trap 6: Treating a due diligence conclusion as a right to judge the other side**
- What it looks like: saying in internal discussions "this one is a scammer" or "this one is definitely no good".
- Why: due diligence conclusions easily pick up emotion.
- The right move: due diligence only answers "is it worth investing time", not "is this person good". Conclusions only record signals and actions — no moral judgments, nothing passed on or spread around.

**Trap 7: Writing internal judgments into `note`, and the script sending them to the customer verbatim**
- What it looks like: writing "looks like a shell company / probably a peer fishing for prices / scale doubtful" in the `note` column during due diligence, then running `outreach_gen.py` and finding "your note mentioned: 疑似皮包" sitting in the English email body.
- Why: not knowing that `outreach_gen.py` pastes `note` verbatim into the outreach email's why-them sentence (see `outreach_gen.py:build_why_them`).
- The right move: `note` carries only **one quotable piece of evidence**, written in English; internal risk judgments go in a `risk` column of your own or an internal notes area. Read every generated English email body before sending.

**Trap 8: Treating "not found" as "does not exist"**
- What it looks like: no employees found on LinkedIn, so writing "no employees = shell company" and killing it outright.
- Why: many small and medium buyers (especially in small countries and family businesses) simply have no LinkedIn company page — not found is not the same as does not exist.
- The right move: always write "not found (channel: LinkedIn)", then cross-check through another channel (the official site's Team page, a trade fair directory, customs records). Only when **several channels all come up empty** does it count as a signal.

## 11. Copy-paste templates (fill in, no AI needed)

## 13. The due diligence agent (script version): `scripts/due_diligence.py`

The twelve sections above are the manual method; this section is how to run it as a script. The script does not go online and does not check anything for you — you feed in the facts you found through public channels, and it judges, grades, and lists the next steps by this page's rules. Python standard library only, runs on Python 3.10+.

**Rules first (so you do not think the script runs a different system):**

- **L1 is the four questions from Section 5 of this page** (website existence / contact details / trading attributes / signs of scale), not three questions. A task brief once wrote L1 as three items (website, years, social media); Section 5 of this page sets four questions — the script follows this page's four questions, which cover the brief's three items and add one more, "contact details" (domain email vs. free mailbox — the first red flag in Section 6).
- **L2 is six dimensions** (identity / scale / reputation / purchasing power / payment habits / compliance), each with only three possible conclusions: pass / to be verified / red flag. What you cannot find is written "to be verified" — never invented.
- **L3 only lists "what to check, where to check it"**, and its status is always "to be checked". Only A-grade is worth those 30 minutes; B/C do not get it.
- **Ratings**: A = all of L1 passed + import records + category match + a specific buyer found, and zero red flags; B = all of L1 passed but evidence is weak, or 1 downgrade red flag hit; C = L1 failed / unanswerable, or 2 or more red flags hit; sanctions list / payment to a third-party account = stop immediately. Rating to T0/T1/T2/T3 mapping: A → T0, B → T1–T2, C → T3.
- **Field names line up with the lead table's 9 columns**: `company` / `country` / `product` / `source` / `email` (in the script called `--email-domain` — fill in only the domain, do not paste a full email) / `phone` / `years` / `contact` (in the script called `--contact-role` — write the role, not a person's name) / `note`. `--note` is one extra line of information from you — shown verbatim, never used in the judgment.
- **Client view** (`--audience client`): only verified public facts + the material you still need the other side to provide. Ratings, red flags, stop status, internal judgments, and raw inputs never leave the building — the same approach as `quote_engine.py`'s client view.
- **JSON output** (`--format json`): the same envelope as `lead_score.py`, with `generated_at` first; the internal version carries `inputs`, the client version only keeps public facts.

### Usage

```bash
# Grade A: an established importer found in customs data
python3 scripts/due_diligence.py --company "[Example] Gulf Tire Trading" \
  --country "Saudi Arabia" --product "TBR 12.00R20" \
  --website sample.example --website-updated recent --years 18 \
  --email-domain sample.example --phone "+000 00 000 0000" \
  --address "Riyadh, Saudi Arabia" --source customs --social active \
  --customs-record yes --import-match yes \
  --contact-role "purchasing manager" --employees 45 \
  --trade-role importer --legal-name-match yes --sanctions clear \
  --adverse no --payment prepaid

# Grade B: a new customer from LinkedIn, evidence still thin
python3 scripts/due_diligence.py --company "[Example] Peninsula Motors" \
  --country "Saudi Arabia" --product "TBR 12.00R20" \
  --website sample.example --years 4 --email-domain sample.example \
  --source linkedin --social active --customs-record unknown \
  --import-match unknown --contact-role "sourcing manager" \
  --employees 6 --trade-role distributor --legal-name-match unknown \
  --sanctions clear --payment unknown

# Grade C: no official site, only a free mailbox, asks for credit terms on first contact
python3 scripts/due_diligence.py --company "[Example] Levant Supply" \
  --country Lebanon --product "TBR 12.00R20" --website none \
  --email-domain gmail.com --source search --social inactive \
  --customs-record no --contact-role "sales" --payment credit

# Only the side the customer gets to see (JSON)
python3 scripts/due_diligence.py --company "[Example] Gulf Tire Trading" \
  --country "Saudi Arabia" --website sample.example --years 18 \
  --email-domain sample.example --source customs --social active \
  --customs-record yes --import-match yes \
  --contact-role "purchasing manager" --format json --audience client

# Write to a file (default only prints to screen)
python3 scripts/due_diligence.py --company "[Example] Gulf Tire Trading" \
  --country "Saudi Arabia" --website sample.example --years 18 \
  --email-domain sample.example --source customs --social active \
  --customs-record yes --import-match yes \
  --contact-role "purchasing manager" --out report.txt
```

`--help` carries the 5 examples above, ready to copy and run. Missing `--company` gives a human-readable error instead of a traceback; three-state parameters like `--customs-record` only accept `yes` / `no` / `unknown`, and a wrong value gets the same human-readable error.

### Three things to look for when reading the report

1. **Look at [Rating] and [Next step] first, then the details.** A means first touch within 48 hours; B means look at [Material still missing], fill in the items in order, then re-grade; C goes straight to the cold pool — do not spend another 30 minutes doing L3 on it.
2. **The English evidence sentence under [Lead table note column] can be pasted straight into the `note` column of `my_leads.csv`** — it only states verified facts and can be said to their face. When nothing was verified, that line is empty — leave it empty, do not invent a sentence (see Trap 8 in Section 10: "not found" ≠ "does not exist").
3. **Internal judgments never go into a file the customer sees.** Ratings, red flags, and stop status in the internal report all disappear when you re-run with `--audience client`; the `note` column that `outreach_gen.py` pastes into the outreach email also only takes that one English evidence sentence — put internal judgments in a `risk` column of your own (see Template 3 in Section 11).

### Common errors

| What you see | What it means | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| `缺 --company：公司名是必填项` | No company name given | Add `--company "company name"`; example companies carry the [Example] prefix |
| `argument --customs-record: invalid choice` | A three-state parameter was written wrong | Only `yes` / `no` / `unknown` are accepted |
| `argument --trade-role: invalid choice: 'unknown'` | The channel role has no `unknown` value | If you do not know, do not pass the parameter — leaving it out means "to be verified" |
| The rating is B, but you think it should be A | One of the A conditions is not met | Look at [Material still missing]: import records, category match, a specific buyer, L2 items to be verified — fill in whichever is missing |
