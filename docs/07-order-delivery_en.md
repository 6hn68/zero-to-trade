# 07 · Order & Delivery

> English version of [07-order-delivery.md](07-order-delivery.md). Translated from the Chinese original; figures, dates, commands and file names match the original exactly.

> **Goal of this stage**: take the order safely to the finish line — sign the right contract, inspect the right goods, collect the right money, deliver the right documents — and turn this order into the entry point for the next one.
> **The principle in one sentence**: in exporting, the money is walked out step by step; it is not talked out at the negotiating table. Miss one step and everything you negotiated so well before it is wasted.

## 1. Why closing the deal is not the finish line

Many people think the job is done once the buyer says OK. Far from it — that is when you start sleeping badly.

The moment an order really makes money is the day the money is in your account, the goods have arrived safely at the port, and the buyer has received them with no complaint. Before that, the money is **accounts receivable**, not profit.

The mistakes beginners most often make all happen in this stretch:

- The contract is signed but the terms are vague (quantity written as "about 500 pcs", delivery written as "ASAP"), and after arrival the buyer says the quantity is wrong and the goods do not match
- You line up full production after receiving a 30% deposit, and then the buyer suddenly says "hold this order for now" or switches supplier
- The goods are finished before you discover the destination port needs a certificate you never knew existed
- One wrong letter in the documents (product name on the invoice, shipping marks, weight) and customs clearance stalls at the port, with yard storage charges piling up every day
- The buyer never confirms the bank receipt / POD, and the balance never comes in

Do not chase speed in this stage; chase one word: explainable. Every action, every document, every payment has to be explainable in front of a third party — when, what, and who confirmed it.

The falls a beginner takes in this stage are often more than in the negotiation stage.

## 2. Step by step (a ten-step closed loop)

> From the buyer saying OK to the money arriving, there are ten steps in total: PI → deposit → production → inspection → balance → booking → customs declaration → bill of lading → clearance documents → after-sales and reorders.
> Run every step through the same three questions: what you have to do / what you have to get / what happens if it gets stuck.

### Step 1: PI and contract (turn a spoken OK into enforceable terms)

Do not mix up the order: quotation → PI (Proforma Invoice — the buyer uses it to pay the deposit, apply for foreign exchange and apply for an import licence) → buyer countersigns → deposit → formal contract (Contract) — or simply treat the countersigned PI as the contract → start production.

- **What you have to do**: write the price, spec, quantity, trade term, delivery time and bank details agreed in 06 into the PI, send it to the buyer, and get the buyer to countersign it or confirm by email ("confirmed"). For small amounts and tight delivery, a countersigned PI commonly serves as the contract; for large amounts or a first order, sign a separate formal contract.
- **What you have to get**: the PI countersigned by the buyer, or the formal contract + the buyer's full company name (identical across the contract, the payer, and the consignee on the B/L) + the spec confirmation sheet as an attachment.
- **What happens if it gets stuck**: if you start production before the PI is confirmed, the buyer can change the spec or cut the quantity later and you have nothing to stand on; if the bank details on the PI are wrong, the money goes to the wrong account and is very hard to chase back.

Clauses the contract must carry (a sample framework — confirm the actual clauses against the real transaction and the law of the buyer's country, and get professional review where needed):

1. **Product name & spec**: model, ply rating, load index, origin, tread pattern, quantity (exact number + the allowed tolerance range, e.g. [Example] ±3%)
2. **Unit price & total price**: state the trade term (`FOB Yantian` or `CIF Dammam`, with the named port) + the price build-up
3. **Quantity & container type**: `1x40HQ, 480 pcs` — write both the piece count and the container count, so the numbers match at loading
4. **Payment terms**: deposit percentage, the point at which the balance is paid, **how the documents control payment** (e.g. payment against a B/L copy / telex release / insurance policy)
5. **Delivery & shipment time**: state the confirmed production time and the shipment time window; do not write "ASAP"
6. **Packing & shipping marks**: packing method, shipping-mark contents, label language requirements (the label content the target market requires is per the latest official rules)
7. **Quality standard & acceptance**: the standard number in force, the claim period (how many days after arrival a complaint may be raised, as actually agreed; [Example] commonly 14–30 days)
8. **Breach & dispute resolution**: liability for breach, force majeure, **where disputes are settled and which law / arbitration body applies**

Signatures and company chops, both companies' full names matching, the contract number, and the attachments (PI, spec confirmation sheet) must all be there. Paying someone to review this step once costs far less than a lawsuit later.

### Step 2: Deposit and payment method (until the money is in, nothing starts)

- **What you have to do**: first set the payment method by risk level (see the table below), then chase the deposit; for any payment proof you receive, **only trust your own bank's credit record**.
- **What you have to get**: the bank credit record (amount, payer name, date); if the payer name does not match the buyer on the contract → ask why before moving.
- **What happens if it gets stuck**: start production before the deposit arrives and one "hold this order for now" from the buyer leaves you holding the inventory and the cash-flow hole.

A bank-receipt screenshot is not proof the money arrived — this is the most common scam.

- Trust the bank's formal receipt / telegraphic transfer advice, not a screenshot the buyer sends you
- Check the account with **your own receiving account and bank name** and confirm the amount actually credited
- A bank receipt can be forged, photoshopped, or someone else's
- The only reliable confirmation is your own bank statement
- One more trap: the buyer's email gets hijacked and a message arrives saying "we have changed bank accounts". Any account change must be double-confirmed through a contact channel you already have (call the buyer's known number / reply inside the original email thread) — do not just reply to that one email

**Payment methods by risk level (safest to most dangerous)**

| Method | Risk | When it can be used | What you have to watch |
|---|---|---|---|
| Advance T/T (100% prepaid before shipment) | Low | New buyer's first order, small amounts | Only start production after the money arrives |
| T/T deposit + balance against B/L copy (paid in full before telex release) | Medium | The mainstream approach after the first order | Only telex-release / send the original B/L after the balance is fully in |
| L/C at sight (issued or confirmed by a bank acceptable to us) | Medium | Buyer insists, larger amounts | **Soft clauses**, see below |
| D/P documents against payment | Medium-high | Old buyers, controllable order value | If the buyer does not pay and does not collect the documents, you have to handle return shipment / resale |
| O/A open account (pay 30/60/90 days after the goods arrive) | High | Only for old buyers you have worked with many times and whose credit can be checked | It is an interest-free loan from you to the buyer; the money sits in someone else's hands |
| Western Union / personal account / third-party payment | Extremely high | Not recommended | Trade background hard to prove, hard to chase when something goes wrong, may touch money laundering |

**Two bottom lines from real practice**

1. **For a new buyer's first order, a 30% deposit is the floor** (50% or 100% is better if you can get it). A 30% deposit is not you being greedy — it is insurance against the buyer vanishing after you have produced. It roughly covers your raw materials and the cost of a spec change — below that number, one buyer default leaves you paying out of your own pocket.
2. Money not in, no production, no document release. This line does not accept "we have a good relationship" or "the buyer is in a hurry" as excuses.

**L/C letter of credit: check three things before accepting one**

1. **Issuing bank and confirmation**: only accept irrevocable. If the issuing bank or the issuing country carries high risk, get the other side to have a bank acceptable to you add confirmation (confirmed).
2. **Soft clauses (the deadliest kind)**: any clause that says "documents signed / countersigned by the buyer or the buyer's representative must be presented before payment can be negotiated" hands the payment switch to the buyer. They typically look like this: an inspection certificate issued by the buyer, the shipment date or vessel name subject to buyer confirmation, samples subject to the buyer's written approval. If the buyer does not sign, your documents are never complete and the bank can refuse payment with a straight face. That line in the L/C saying "inspection certificate signed by the buyer required" looks like a procedure; in fact it hands the safety switch back to the buyer. When you hit a soft clause, either delete it or change it to an independent third-party body issuing the document.
3. **Document requirements and deadlines**: can you actually prepare every document inside the shipment period and the presentation period? The expiry place is best set in China.

The "bank credit" in a letter of credit only holds when the documents match. If the documents do not match, the bank refuses payment, no discussion. It protects you against a buyer who refuses to pay out of bad faith; it does not protect you against your own document mistakes.

### Step 3: Production (turn progress into evidence)

- **What you have to do**: confirm the production schedule and the key milestones, record the raw-material batches (rubber / steel cord / fabric batches affect consistency), and ask for progress photos or video at each milestone (not verbal assurances).
- **What you have to get**: production-progress photos/video, the completion notice, the actual shippable quantity and the loading plan.
- **What happens if it gets stuck**: with no progress evidence, when there is a delay you cannot explain whether it is the factory's problem or yours; if you discover mid-way that the line was switched or batches were mixed, the whole lot has to be reworked.
- The moment you see a delay coming, notify the buyer in writing at once (the how is in "What to do when something goes wrong" below). Silence is the most expensive response in this business.

### Step 4: Inspection (stop the risk before loading)

- **Mid-production inspection**: when bulk production reaches 30%–50%, ask the factory for video or photos
- Pre-loading inspection (this step matters most, bar none):
  - Do it yourself or hire a third-party inspection body (the cost is usually very low against the order value, but it blocks most problems)
  - Must-check items: model matches the contract, tread pattern correct, appearance defects (bubbles / impurities / cracks), packing and shipping marks correct, label contents, sample-weighing
  - Photograph: the loading process, close-ups of the shipping marks, close-ups of random sample checks, the container number after loading is complete
  - When a problem shows up: demand rework or sorting on the spot; do not gamble on "a small difference the buyer will probably accept"
- **Certificates**: the certification certificates and inspection certificates the buyer or the destination country requires — get them before loading and check the numbers match the contract (**the exact certification requirements are per the latest official rules**)
- **What you have to get**: the inspection report + the photo/video pack (loading process, shipping-mark close-ups, sample-check close-ups, container number and seal number) + the actual piece count after loading
- What happens if it gets stuck: skip this step and every dispute after arrival turns into "your word against theirs" — with no evidence you can only pay up.

### Step 5: Balance payment (payment against documents; no release until the money is complete)

- **What you have to do**: after shipment, send the buyer the B/L copy / insurance policy, collect the balance at the contract point, and only telex-release or send the original B/L after confirming the money is in.
- **What you have to get**: bank-statement confirmation that the balance arrived; a telex release needs the buyer's written confirmation (an email is enough).
- What happens if it gets stuck: release the documents or telex-release before the money is fully in, and the goods and the money are both gone — the cleanest way to lose money in exporting.
- **What to do if the buyer refuses or drags the balance**: keep the contract, PI, shipping notice, B/L, invoice and chat records, **keep holding the title to the goods** (the original B/L stays in your hands), and chase in writing with a deadline. This is the entire reason "deposit first + control the documents" exists.

### Step 6: Booking (lock the space early)

- **What you have to do**: get quotes from forwarders — ask at least 3 (price, sailing schedule, direct or transshipment, whether destination-port charges are included); confirm the sailing schedule and the cut-off times, work backwards from them to the production completion date, and set the delivery date by working backwards; after loading, check the **actual loaded quantity, container number and seal number** and photograph them for the record.
- **What you have to get**: the booking confirmation / Shipping Order (SO), vessel name and voyage, cut-off and documentation cut-off times, photos of the container number and seal number.
- **What happens if it gets stuck**: peak-season space shortage / rolled bookings → missed delivery and breach of contract; no photo of the container and seal numbers → you cannot explain a short-shipment dispute later.

### Step 7: Customs declaration (export filing — do not leave it to the last minute)

- **What you have to do**: hand the contract, invoice, packing list and the other declaration documents to the customs broker / forwarder, and confirm the HS code and the declaration elements.
- **What you have to get**: the customs declaration form or release notice, and HS code confirmation; where export tax refunds or foreign-exchange receipts are involved, keep the documents per the latest official rules.
- **What happens if it gets stuck**: a wrong HS code or declaration element → inspection, amendment, missed sailing; incomplete documents → follow-on problems with the tax refund and foreign-exchange procedures.

### Step 8: Bill of lading (the title document — check it word by word)

- **What you have to do**: when you receive the draft B/L, **check it word by word**: shipper, consignee, notify party, product name, number of packages, gross weight, volume, freight terms, destination port, on-board date.
- **What you have to get**: the original B/L (or telex-release confirmation) + the final B/L scan.
- **What happens if it gets stuck**: one wrong letter can mean an amendment, an amendment fee, or blocked clearance; whether the consignee is straight (named) or to order directly decides whether you still control the goods — if unsure, ask the forwarder; do not assume. Amending before the cut-off is cheap; after loading it is expensive and slow; at the destination port it is almost impossible.

### Step 9: Clearance document pack (so the buyer can actually pick up the goods)

Delivery is not the goods reaching the port — it is the buyer being able to take them out smoothly. Send the buyer the full document set within 24 hours of shipment, and confirm he received it.

| Document | Issued by | When | Sent to | What goes wrong if it is wrong |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Commercial Invoice | You | Around shipment | Customs declaration + buyer clearance | Amount / product name mismatch, customs valuation stuck |
| Packing List | You | Around shipment | Customs declaration + buyer clearance | Piece count / gross weight mismatch with the B/L, inspection triggered |
| B/L / Sea Waybill | Shipping line / forwarder | After loading | Buyer, for pickup | Wrong shipper / consignee, goods cannot be collected |
| Insurance Certificate | You (under CIF) | Insured before loading | Buyer, for claims | Cover / insured amount too low, cargo damage not compensated |
| Certificate of Origin | Issuing bodies such as CCPIT / customs (per the latest official rules) | Around shipment | Buyer clearance / tariffs | Missing or wrong information, clearance stuck or extra duty paid |
| Inspection certificate / third-party inspection report | Third-party body | Around loading | Buyer / L/C negotiation | Missing certificate under an L/C = the bank may refuse payment |
| Mandatory destination-country certification (e.g. GSO standards for Gulf countries, Saudi SABER / SASO, etc.) | Designated body | **Before loading** | Buyer clearance | Only discovering at arrival that a certificate is needed → return shipment or abandoned goods |
| Shipping advice / beneficiary's certificate (often required under an L/C) | You | As the L/C requires | Buyer / bank | Documents not matching → the bank may refuse payment |

The three-match rule: the product name, piece count and gross weight on the invoice, packing list and B/L must be completely identical. This is the bottom line for customs clearance and L/C negotiation — memorize it.

**The destination-port side (remind the buyer; do not handle it for him, but cooperate)**

- Send the buyer the B/L copy, invoice, packing list, certificate of origin and certification documents in advance, so his clearance can start early
- Remind the buyer to check the destination country's import licence, clearance-agent arrangements, and certification and labelling requirements (**per the latest official announcements**)
- After arrival, follow up proactively: ask whether clearance went smoothly and unloading was normal

### Appendix: what to do when something goes wrong (four common exceptions, can happen at any step from step 3 to step 9)

**1) Delays (production overrun / rolled booking / sailing delayed)**

- Principle: you say it first; do not let the buyer hear it from someone else.
- Actions: once you have a firm new date → notify in writing (cause + new date + what you have already done) → give the buyer options (ship the ready part first / wait for the full container / change sailing).
- Do not: sit on it without saying anything, answer "almost there, almost there", or invent a reason that gets exposed. A buyer can accept a delay; what he struggles to accept is being kept in the dark.

**2) Short / over shipment**

- First check the tolerance clause in the contract (e.g. [Example] ±3%): settle on the actual quantity if inside the range; only discuss options if outside it.
- Evidence: packing list, weighbridge records, loading photos, container number and **seal number** (if the seal is intact and the number matches, the responsibility is usually not yours).
- Do not: the moment the buyer says some are missing, immediately send replacements — check the evidence first.

**3) Quality complaints**

- First response within 24–48 hours: first reply "received, we are checking" — before you have evidence, do not admit liability.
- Ask the buyer for: photos / video, the batch number (production week code on the sidewall), how many pieces were sampled and in what proportion, what share of pieces has the problem.
- Follow the claim period in the contract; arrange a third-party re-inspection where needed.
- Solutions come in steps — cost them before promising: discount on the next order → replacement goods → partial compensation → full compensation. Admitting liability first sends every buyer the signal: this supplier pays out whenever goods have a problem.

**4) Cargo damage / port detention / refusal**

- CIF, insured by you: notify the insurer / forwarder immediately, fix the evidence (arrival photos, packing list, B/L, damage records), and claim through the insurance.
- FOB: risk and title have transferred after loading; cooperate by providing documents, but **do not take on liability that is not yours**.
- Refusal / detention at port: first establish who is responsible under the contract and the insurance terms, then discuss return shipment, resale or discounted disposal; dragging the decision out only lets storage charges keep rising.

### Step 10: After-sales and reorders (the real point of this order)

Closing the deal is not the finish line; it is the starting point of the next order.

- 3–7 days after delivery, proactively ask once how the goods are being used / whether they arrived fine (not to chase the next order — to make the buyer feel you are still there)
- Keep a buyer file: usual models, average monthly container volume, decision maker, payment habits, preferences on quality / delivery time, who his downstream buyers are
- Work out his reorder rhythm in advance: tires are consumables; estimate from the buyer's actual sales and restocking cycle, and come to him 2–3 weeks before his restocking point.
- Set a reorder reminder: mark in the calendar "Buyer A · expected restocking window · contact 3 weeks early" — do not wait for him to remember on his own
- Actively ask for referrals: when the buyer is happy, ask directly "do you know anyone else in your market looking for this spec?" It is the cheapest way to get customers, and referred buyers start out trusting you more.
- **Turn this order into evidence**: build one case you can tell publicly — market + buyer type + spec shipped + delivery cycle + what problem it solved. **Anonymize everything public**: no real company names, personal names, emails or phone numbers — write it as "a wholesaler at a Middle Eastern port · 2 containers a month" and the like

## 3. Pass criteria

The hard bars for "this stage is done":

- The contract is signed, both companies' full names match, and it does not contradict the quotation / PI / spec sheet in any of the three places, and it has been professionally reviewed (large amounts / first orders).
- The payment method has been chosen by risk level: first-order deposit not below 30%, not O/A, not a personal-account payment; the deposit is confirmed credited by **our own bank statement**.
- If L/C: confirmed irrevocable, issuing bank acceptable (confirmation added where needed), **soft clauses already demanded to be deleted or issued by a third party**.
- The balance-payment point and the document-control method are clear; documents are only released / telex-released after the money is confirmed in; any bank-account change has been double-confirmed through the old contact channel.
- Pre-loading inspection is done and photo/video evidence kept; mandatory destination-country certification and certificate numbers were obtained **before loading** and match the contract.
- Declaration documents and the HS code are confirmed; invoice, packing list and B/L match on product name / piece count / gross weight.
- The draft B/L has been checked word by word; the full document set was sent to the buyer within 24h of shipment and receipt confirmed.
- When a delay or exception happened, the buyer was notified in writing with a record kept; the reorder reminder, the referral request and the anonymized case are on file.

## 4. AI prompts

**Prompt 1: Contract-clause self-check**

```
You are an international trade documentation assistant (NOT a lawyer — flag anything that needs
professional legal review).

I am a Chinese tire exporter reviewing a sales contract. Review my draft terms against the deal
facts and output a risk checklist.

DEAL FACTS:
- Buyer: {company, country}
- Product & spec: {size, pattern, ply, load index, origin}
- Quantity: {pcs} in {container type}
- Price & term: {unit price, total, FOB/CIF + named port}
- Payment: {deposit %, balance trigger, document control}
- Delivery: {production window, shipment window}
- Incoterms version agreed with buyer: {version}

DEAL CONTEXT I KNOW: {e.g. "new buyer, first order, 30% deposit, CIF Dammam",
"buyer usually pushes back on inspection timeline"}

OUTPUT — a numbered list of RED FLAGS grouped as:
1. AMBIGUOUS TERMS — wording that could be read two ways. Quote the clause, show the two readings.
   Especially check: quantity wording (exact vs "about"), delivery dates, packing/labeling
   specification, "acceptable quality" definitions.
2. MISSING TERMS — clauses international sales contracts normally have that mine omits.
3. INTERNAL MISMATCHES — where the contract contradicts my quote, PI, or the technical spec sheet.
4. COLLECTION RISKS — specifically assess: what happens if the buyer refuses the balance payment,
   or if the goods turn out different from what was agreed. Does the contract give me the
   document control I need?
5. JURISDICTION / DISPUTE — note that governing law and dispute forum must be chosen deliberately
   and may need legal advice.
6. ITEMS TO CONFIRM WITH THE BUYER — a short list of questions to send before signing.

RULES: Do not draft contract language for me. Do not invent legal statutes or article numbers.
Give me the risk and the question, not a fake clause. State clearly that contract language and
dispute resolution need professional review.
```

In plain words: the most useful part is section 1, "one sentence, two readings" — for example "quantity: about 480 pcs", where the other side can argue that 400 is within a reasonable range. The opening line declares it is not a lawyer and gives no clause text, only risks and questions, so it does not invent statutes.

   which documents I will provide and when, and explicitly stating that goods/documents are

```

In plain words: this one is about fraud prevention. Section 1 specifically names the fact that "a telegraphic-transfer receipt can be forged", and section 2 gives actions you can actually execute instead of empty advice like "verify carefully". The last section, section 5, hands you a paragraph of payment instructions you can send to the buyer directly.

## 5. Checklist

- [ ] Contract signed, both companies' full names match, and the clauses cover: spec, quantity (with tolerance range), unit price + total price, trade term + named port, payment terms, delivery window, packing and shipping marks, acceptance standard and claim period, dispute resolution
- [ ] Contract checked: completely consistent with the quotation, PI and technical spec sheet in all three places, no contradictions
- [ ] Contract reviewed by a professional (e.g. large amount or first time working with a buyer from that country)
- [ ] Payment method set by risk level: first-order deposit ≥30%, no O/A / personal account / third-party payment
- [ ] If L/C: confirmed irrevocable, issuing bank acceptable (or confirmation added), soft clauses already demanded to be deleted
- [ ] Deposit arrival confirmed by **our own bank statement**, not a screenshot the buyer provided; payer matches the buyer on the contract
- [ ] If the bank account changed, double-confirmed through the old contact channel (phone / original email thread)
- [ ] Balance-payment point and document-control method are clear (B/L copy / telex release / insurance policy), and documents are only released after the money is confirmed in
- [ ] Progress photos or video received during production, raw-material / production-line information recorded
- [ ] Pre-loading inspection done, model, pattern, appearance, packing, labels and weight checked item by item, with photo/video evidence kept
- [ ] All required certificates obtained, certificate numbers matching the contract
- [ ] Mandatory destination-country certification (e.g. GSO / SABER / SASO, **per the latest official rules**) obtained before loading
- [ ] Declaration documents and HS code confirmed, customs declaration form / release notice on file
- [ ] Invoice, packing list and B/L completely identical on product name / piece count / gross weight
- [ ] Draft B/L checked word by word: product name, package count, gross weight, ports, shipper and consignee, freight terms
- [ ] Full document set sent to the buyer in advance (invoice, packing list, B/L copy, certificate of origin, certificates), confirmed not blocking his clearance
- [ ] Loading photos, seal number and container number kept on file
- [ ] If a delay or exception happened, the buyer was notified in writing (cause + new date + measures) with a record kept
- [ ] Buyer file updated, **reorder reminder written into the calendar** (2–3 weeks before the restocking window)
- [ ] Referral request made to a satisfied buyer; this order's case anonymized and filed (no real company names / personal names / contact details)

## 6. Common beginner traps

**Trap 1: Trusting a bank-receipt screenshot**
- What it looks like: the buyer sends a bank transfer screenshot — amount right, letterhead right, stamp looks real — so you start production. Days later the money still has not arrived.
- Why: bank receipts, photoshopped images and forged telegraphic-transfer advices can all be made, and made to look very convincing.
- The right move: the only valid confirmation is your own bank's credit record. Whatever payment proof you receive, check it against your own account at the bank. When the buyer pushes you with "start production first, the money is on its way" — that is textbook scam wording.

**Trap 2: Writing the contract vaguely**
- What it looks like: quantity written as "about 500 pcs", delivery as "ASAP", pattern as "regular model".
- Why: avoiding hassle, assuming the other side will not take advantage.
- The right move: numbers must be exact with a tolerance range agreed; delivery written as a clear window; pattern and ply rating written into the spec sheet as a contract attachment. These three are where disputes concentrate afterwards.

**Trap 3: Loading without inspection**
- What it looks like: skipping inspection to save time and money, trusting the factory's "no problem".
- Why: treating the inspection cost as waste.
- The right move: pre-loading inspection plus photo/video evidence is the best-value step in the whole process. One third-party inspection usually costs far less than sorting out a dispute after a shipment arrives at port, plus the reputation damage.

**Trap 4: Not checking the draft B/L**
- What it looks like: the draft B/L arrives, you glance at it and reply OK.
- Why: not knowing the B/L is the key document controlling title to the goods.
- The right move: check shipper, consignee, product name, package count, gross weight, destination port and freight terms word by word. Fixing a problem before the cut-off costs very little; fixing it after loading is expensive, and sometimes impossible.

**Trap 5: Documents delayed, buyer stuck at the port**
- What it looks like: the goods have arrived, but the buyer says the invoice and packing list have not arrived and clearance is impossible, while yard storage charges keep rising.
- Why: you stopped managing after shipment and forgot the buyer also needs your documents to clear customs.
- The right move: send the buyer the full document set within 24 hours of shipment and confirm he received it. Delivery is not finished when the goods reach the port — it is finished when the buyer can clear customs smoothly.

**Trap 6: Going silent after closing**
- What it looks like: after shipping you wait for the next order, and do nothing unless the buyer comes to you.
- Why: treating the closed deal as the finish line.
- The right move: proactively ask once after delivery how the goods are being used, then work out the buyer's restocking window and contact him 2–3 weeks before it. Tires are consumables; reorders happen naturally — the question is whether they come to you or to someone else.

**Trap 7: Compensating the buyer too readily**
- What it looks like: the buyer says the goods have a problem, and you agree to a refund or replacement before verifying anything.
- Why: fear of angering the buyer, fear of losing the order.
- The right move: follow the claim period in the contract — first ask for photos / sampling records, arrange a third-party re-inspection where needed, and only discuss solutions once responsibility is clear. Admitting liability first sends every buyer the signal: this supplier pays out whenever goods have a problem.

**Trap 8: Letting the buyer dictate the payment method**
- What it looks like: the buyer says "we always do O/A 90 days" and you agree, because you are afraid of losing the order.
- Why: treating payment terms as a bargaining chip instead of a risk exposure.
- The right move: no credit terms for new buyers; the first-order floor is a 30% deposit, with the balance paid against the B/L copy before documents are released. Credit terms are bought with your cash flow — give them only to old buyers you have worked with many times whose credit can be checked, and build the funding cost back into the price. Western Union, personal accounts and third-party payments: never accept them.

**Trap 9: Relaxing the moment you get a letter of credit**
- What it looks like: the buyer says "we will open an L/C, the bank guarantees it, don't worry", and you stop reading the clauses.
- Why: assuming a letter of credit means the bank pays unconditionally.
- The right move: the bank only pays when the documents match. Check every clause for soft clauses (the kind that needs an inspection certificate issued by the buyer, or shipment confirmed by the buyer) — if any exist, demand they be deleted or changed to a third-party body; if the issuing bank or country carries high risk, demand confirmation.

**Trap 10: Confirming a bank-account change with a single email reply**
- What it looks like: a message arrives saying "we have changed bank accounts, please pay to the new one", and you change it and send.
- Why: the email looks completely normal, and the sender really is the buyer.
- The right move: any change to a receiving account must be double-confirmed through a contact channel you already have (call the buyer's known number / reply inside the original email thread). A hijacked mailbox changing the receiving account is one of the most common telegraphic-transfer frauds in exporting.

**Trap 11: Hiding a delay**
- What it looks like: production slips, a booking gets rolled, and you choose not to tell the buyer yet, hoping to make it up later.
- Why: fear of angering the buyer.
- The right move: the moment you judge there will be a delay, notify in writing at once: cause + a firm new date + what you have already done, then give the buyer options. A buyer can accept a delay; what he struggles to accept is being kept in the dark — hiding it costs more than this one order.

Delay notice (send it the same day you know there will be a delay — do not wait):

Subject: Update on shipment for Contract No. ___ — new ETD

Hi ___,

```

## 8. Back to step one

After seven stages, you have your first batch of closed buyers and your first batch of mistakes stepped in.

Now go back to **[01 · Market Selection](01-market-selection_en.md)**: you now have real data on how big this market is, which price levels work, which models are in demand, and which step jams most easily. Use this real feedback to correct your earlier judgment, then decide where the second market is.

- Certifications, documents and payment methods you actually used in a given market — add them into the matching stage document
- The mistakes you stepped in, and the real story of a refused payment / a claim — write them into "Common beginner traps"
- Templates you think are missing, or better AI prompts — add them directly

The workflow only really starts turning here — it is not a straight road, it is a loop that gets a little more accurate each round. Every round you finish, the next person steps in one fewer trap.
